Regression and an influential point: move an observation

In this example, adding one distant point makes the correlation r (unitless, from −1 to 1: ±1 for perfectly aligned points, 0 for no linear relationship) fall from 0.948 to 0.348. You get into the habit of looking at the scatter before concluding.

A course experiment whose values are computed within the stated model and are not research findings: The chapter: summary, formulas and FAQ (slides in French) · All experiments · Glossary: influential point.

The Student t test on the slope, with an extreme point

Is the slope a of the line different from 0? The Student t test divides a by its standard error σ̂ₐ. Move the extreme patient: p goes above or below 0.05.

Show the formulas for the correlation, the line and the tests

cov(X,Y)=1n−1∑i=1n(xi−X¯)(yi−Y¯)

r=cov(X,Y)sXsY

Y^=aX+b,a=cov(X,Y)sX2,b=Y¯−aX¯

ei=yi−y^i,SCR=∑i=1nei2,σ^2=SCRn−2

SCY=∑i=1n(yi−Y¯)2,SCY^=∑i=1n(y^i−Y¯)2

R2=SCY^SCY=SCY−SCRSCY=r2

t=rn−21−r2andT=aσ^a,σ^a=σ^2∑i=1n(xi−X¯)2

Where: n is the number of pairs (xᵢ, yᵢ); X̄ and Ȳ the means, sₓ and sᵧ the standard deviations of X and Y; cov(X,Y) the covariance; r the Pearson correlation (unitless, between −1 and 1); a the slope and b the intercept of the least-squares line Ŷ; ŷᵢ the value of the line at xᵢ and eᵢ the residual; SCR the residual sum of squares and σ̂² the residual variance; SCY the total variability of Y and SCŶ the part explained by the line; t the statistic of the test on r, T that of the slope test and σ̂ₐ the standard error of the slope.

Both statistics follow the Student law with n − 2 degrees of freedom (df) and have the same value: H₀ (no link, a = 0) is rejected if |t| exceeds the table t (2.101 for 18 df at the 5% risk).

One single test. In simple linear regression, testing the correlation r and testing the slope a is the same test: both t statistics are equal and share the same p-value (as the course notes, this no longer holds in multiple regression).

The intercept b and centring. The model Ŷ = aX + b contains a constant b = Ȳ − aX̄. It vanishes (b = 0) when both X and Y are centred (their mean is subtracted): the line then goes through the origin, which is the mean point (X̄, Ȳ). Centring only X gives b = Ȳ; centring only Y gives b = −aX̄: in both cases b is not zero in general. Centring changes neither the slope, nor r, nor R², nor the test. If the variables are also divided by their standard deviations (standardized variables), the slope becomes r: this is the “Beta (β)” coefficient of the software shown in the course.

Reading the p-value. State a null hypothesis H₀ and an alternative H₁. Under H₀, the p-value p is the probability of a test statistic at least as extreme as the one observed. Set the threshold α in advance, often at 5%, then reject H₀ when p < α.

What does the α threshold control?

Under H₀ and the test assumptions, α limits the false rejection rate over repeated experiments. Interpret a significant result alongside the effect size and its uncertainty.

Exercises

Taken from the course and its tutorials (no exam paper).

  1. Correlation and extreme point (course). With the extreme point at the top (age 65, score 25), then at the bottom (age 65, score 12), note r, t and p. Conclude.
    Show the answer

    Top: r = −0.075, t = −0.32, p = 0.75, not significant. Bottom: r = −0.464, t = −2.22 (18 df, table t 2.101), p = 0.040, significant. A single patient changes the conclusion (slide 32 reports r magnitudes of 0.071 and 0.462, with p = 0.765 and 0.040; the reconstructed cloud gives similar values).

  2. Blood pressure and age (course). For 34 patients, the line is Pressure = 60.3928 + 1.5771 × age and r = 0.7868; the residual sum of squares is SCR = 2605.569. Patient no. 5 is 44 years old with a pressure of 144. Compute the predicted pressure, the residual, σ̂², r² and test r.
    Show the answer

    ŷ₅ = 60.3928 + 1.5771 × 44 = 129.79; residual e₅ = 144 − 129.79 = 14.21 (point above the line); σ̂² = 2605.569/32 = 81.42; r² = 0.619: the line explains 61.9% of the total variability of the pressure. t = 0.7868 × √32/√(1 − 0.619) = 7.21 with 32 df (table t ≈ 2.04): significant correlation, p < 0.0001.

  3. Centring. Without the extreme point: X̄ = 41.05 years, Ȳ = 16.68 and the slope a = −0.0719. What is b if only X is centred, only Y, then both?
    Show the answer

    Raw: b = Ȳ − aX̄ = 19.64. X centred alone: b = Ȳ = 16.68. Y centred alone: b = −aX̄ = 2.95. Both centred: b = 0. X and Y must both be centred to remove the constant.

Try it and interpret

Move the orange point, then turn it off. Compare the lines, r and the p-value (the probability of an association at least this strong if the two quantities were unrelated). Then place it far away in x but aligned with the others, then off the line: the second case is the one that changes everything.

How can one observation change the fitted line, correlation and p-value?

Least squares penalize squared differences: a point far away in x has high leverage and can pull the line toward it. If it also departs from the relationship, the slope, r and the p-value change strongly. Far away but aligned, it changes the line little.

Show the formulas

b1=cov(x,y)sx2

b0=y¯−b1x¯

r=cov(x,y)sxsy

Where: b₁ is the slope and b₀ the intercept of the least-squares line, x̄ and ȳ the means, sₓ and sᵧ the standard deviations of x and y, cov(x, y) their covariance and r the Pearson correlation.

Definitions

b₁, b₀
The slope and the intercept of the least-squares line.
x̄, ȳ
The means of x and y.
sₓ, sᵧ
The standard deviations of x and y.
cov(x, y)
The covariance: how x and y vary together.
r
Pearson correlation. See correlation, significance and R².
Leverage
The potential weight of a point on the line, greater the farther it lies in x.

Assumptions and limitations

Teaching points, not research data. Always inspect the scatter and residuals: a coefficient alone does not reveal an influential point.
