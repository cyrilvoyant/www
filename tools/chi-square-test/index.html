Chi-square test: observed counts, expected counts and contributions

Over 120 throws of a die, a single face appearing 68 times instead of 20 contributes 115.2 of the 138.5 points of χ². You learn to look for where the departure lies, instead of stopping at the p-value.

An experiment from Cyril Voyant’s course, running in your browser. The chapter: summary, formulas and FAQ (slides in French) · All experiments.

Try it and interpret

For 120 throws of a fair die, expect 20 occurrences of each face. Change one face and inspect its contribution. The 2×2 table uses the course case 75/25 and 65/35: p ≈ 0.1228 without continuity correction, so no rejection at the 5% level.

Which cells contribute to the discrepancy between observations and the hypothesis?

Each cell contributes (O−E)²/E, and χ² is their sum. Reading these contributions shows where the departure lies, which the p-value does not say. For an equal difference, a cell with a small expected count weighs more.

χ2=∑(O−E)2E

Fair die: E = N/6, 5 degrees of freedom. Independence in a 2×2 table: E = (row total × column total)/N, 1 degree of freedom.

Definitions

O, E
The observed count and the count expected under the tested hypothesis.
N
The total count.
Σ
The sum over all cells.
χ²
The test statistic: the sum of the contributions (O−E)²/E.
Degrees of freedom
The parameter of the reference distribution: 5 for the die, 1 for the 2×2 table.
p-value
The probability of exceeding the χ² obtained if the hypothesis is true, under the approximate distribution. A small p-value says the departure is hardly compatible with the hypothesis, not that it is large: see the course chapter Testing a difference and reading its uncertainty.

Assumptions and limitations

Independent observations, mutually exclusive categories, actual counts. The p-value is approximate: with small expected counts, the automatic conclusion is suspended. Failing to reject demonstrates neither fairness nor independence.
