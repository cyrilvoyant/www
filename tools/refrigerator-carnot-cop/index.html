Refrigeration: coefficient of performance and Carnot limit

Between −10 °C and +40 °C, a refrigerator extracts at best 5.26 J of heat per joule of work (its COP, coefficient of performance); between −40 °C and +20 °C, at best 3.89 J. This theoretical maximum is the Carnot limit. You understand why producing cold costs more as the temperature gap widens.

A course experiment whose values are computed within the stated model and are not research findings: The chapter and its slides (French) · All experiments · Glossary: COP.

Try it and interpret

Cold reservoir at −10 °C and hot reservoir at +40 °C: maximum COP = 263.15/(313.15−263.15) ≈ 5.26 (temperatures in kelvins: °C + 273.15). With −40 °C and +20 °C: 233.15/60 ≈ 3.89. Lower the cold reservoir or raise the hot one and follow the point on the curve: the COP falls.

Why does a larger temperature difference make refrigeration more demanding?

A refrigerator pumps heat from cold to hot, and the wider the gap between the reservoirs, the more work each joule extracted requires. The Carnot limit quantifies this cost from the absolute temperatures: a 50 K gap in the first example, 60 K in the second.

Temperatures in kelvins. The Carnot COP is the theoretical maximum, not a percentage efficiency.

Definitions

COP
The coefficient of performance: heat extracted from the cold reservoir per unit of work supplied.
Tcold, Thot
The temperatures of the cold and hot reservoirs, in kelvins.
Carnot limit
The maximum COP of a reversible machine between these two reservoirs. See the Carnot engine.
COP > 1
Normal: COP compares heat moved with work; it is not a percentage efficiency.

Assumptions and limitations

Reversible machine between two reservoirs: a bound, not the actual COP of equipment. Fans and losses are not modeled; temperatures are those of the reservoirs, not of the refrigerant.
