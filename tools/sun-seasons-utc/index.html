The Sun, the seasons and the clock: one time for every measurement

On 21 June 2026, the day lasts 16 h 11 in Paris and 9 h 54 in Sydney. At the same instant, 12:00 UTC, it is 14:00 in Paris and 22:00 in Sydney. Solar irradiance follows two cycles at once, the day and the year, and these cycles do not fall at the same hour from one place to another: to compare and align data, one clock is needed, Coordinated Universal Time (UTC).

In brief

  • The seasons come from the tilt of the Earth's axis (23.44°), not from its distance to the Sun: on 21 June 2026, the day lasts 16 h 11 in Paris and 9 h 54 in Sydney.
  • Solar noon comes 4 minutes earlier per degree of longitude to the east: on 21 June, near 11:52 UTC in Paris and 01:56 UTC in Sydney.
  • To align measurements: timestamp in UTC (ISO 8601), state whether the stamp marks the start, middle or end of the period, check on a clear-sky day.

1. Around the Sun: the seasons

The Earth's rotation axis is tilted by 23.44° and keeps the same direction in space all year. In June the North Pole leans towards the Sun: the northern hemisphere gets higher and longer sunshine, it is summer; in December it is the reverse, and summer is in Sydney.

The latitude where the Sun is At the zenith: the point of the sky straight above the place. at noon is the declination δ; it goes from +23.44° (June Day when the declination reaches its extreme, +23.44° or −23.44°: around 21 June and 21 December.) to −23.44° (December solstice). Distance plays no part: the Earth is closest to the Sun in early January, in the middle of the northern winter.

The tilt acts in two ways that add up. Longer days (view 2): when the axis leans towards the Sun, the circle of latitude of Paris spends more than half its turn in the light, that of Sydney less; at the Day when the declination is zero: the Sun is overhead at the equator, in March and in September. every circle is half lit, 12 h.

A higher Sun (sketch under view 1): the same 1 m² beam of rays covers 1.11 m² of ground in Paris on 21 June (Sun at 65°) but 1.85 m² in Sydney (Sun at 33°): each square meter receives less. More time and more energy per square meter: that is summer.

2. Rotation: day and night

The Earth turns by 15° per hour. Solar noon, when the Sun crosses the local Imaginary half-circle joining the two poles through the place; the Sun reaches the highest point of its daily path there., therefore comes 4 minutes earlier per degree of longitude to the east. In Paris (2.35° E) it falls around 11:52 UTC on 21 June, 13:52 on the clock; in Sydney (151.2° E) around 01:56 UTC, 11:56 on the clock.

Up to a few minutes (the Difference between true solar noon and the noon of a fictitious Sun moving at constant speed; it comes from the elliptical orbit and the tilt of the axis., from −14 to +16 minutes over the year), noon is the same nowhere: the map shows the lit area and the point where the Sun is overhead.

3. Day length

When the Sun is on the side of the place's hemisphere, the day exceeds 12 hours, all the more the further the place is from the equator; on the opposite side, it is shorter.

On 21 June the day lasts 16 h 11 in Paris (sunrise 05:46, sunset 21:58, summer time) and 9 h 54 in Sydney; on 21 December, 8 h 15 in Paris and 14 h 25 in Sydney.

In UTC, Sydney's day straddles two dates: on 21 June the Sun rises there on 20 June at 20:59 UTC.

4. The double seasonality of irradiance

Outside the atmosphere, on a horizontal plane, Power of solar radiation received per square meter of surface, in watts per square meter (W/m²). is G0 = Gsc·E0·cos θz, with Gsc = 1361 W/m² the Mean irradiance outside the atmosphere, facing the Sun, at the mean Earth-Sun distance; 1361 W/m² according to Kopp and Lean (2011)., E0 = 1 + 0.033·cos(2π n/365) the distance factor on day n of the year, and θz the angle between the vertical and the Sun.

It carries two cycles: the daily cycle (rotation) and the yearly cycle (tilt), which modulates the height and length of the first.

On the heat maps each column is a day and each row a UTC hour: the bright band of Paris is centered near 12 h UTC, that of Sydney near 2 h UTC, cut in two by UTC midnight; one widens in June, the other in December.

Forecasting methods use this structure, for instance through the ratio to the clear-sky trend (the signal divided by that of a cloudless sky; clear-sky index) or BLEND, a Weighted mean whose weights are non-negative and sum to 1: the result stays between the two forecasts combined. of persistence (copying the last known value) and cyclic persistence (copying the value of the day before at the same time).

Once the cycle is removed, what remains is measured by sCV (its variability) and F (its forecastability). Which forecast for which signal?

5. Timestamping measurements: one clock

The time on a country's clocks: UTC plus an offset set by that country, which may change in summer. is UTC plus an offset that changes twice a year, on dates that differ between countries: Paris is UTC+1 in winter and UTC+2 in summer, Sydney UTC+10 in the austral winter and UTC+11 in the austral summer.

A series stamped in legal time therefore loses one hour in spring and sees one twice in autumn, and two stations in different countries are not aligned. The tables below show it, half-hour by half-hour, around three changes of 2026.

  • Timestamp in UTC (International standard for writing dates and times, from the year to the second; the final Z means UTC., for instance 2026-10-25T06:00Z) and keep the site longitude to move to solar time.
  • State whether the stamp marks the start, the middle or the end of the averaging period: a half-period shift moves the whole daily curve and biases the comparison with a model.
  • Check the alignment on a clear-sky day: the measured maximum must fall near the computed solar noon.

Words of the page

Declination δ
latitude where the Sun is overhead at noon; from −23.44° to +23.44° over the year.
Equation of time
difference, from −14 to +16 minutes, between true solar noon and the noon of a mean Sun; due to the elliptical orbit and the tilt.
True solar time, hour angle
true solar time is UTC + equation of time + 4 min per degree of east longitude; the hour angle is zero at solar noon and grows by 15° per hour.
Zenith angle θz
angle between the vertical and the direction of the Sun; cos θz = sin φ sin δ + cos φ cos δ cos h, with φ the latitude, δ the declination and h the hour angle.
Distance factor E0
correction for the Earth-Sun distance: 1 at the mean distance, 1.033 in early January, 0.967 in early July.
Refraction
the atmosphere bends the rays: the Sun stays visible a little after its geometric sunset. Hence a few extra minutes of daylight.
UTC
Coordinated Universal Time: one clock for the whole Earth, without summer time.
Legal time
UTC plus an offset set by each country, which may change in summer.
G0
extraterrestrial solar irradiance on a horizontal plane: a bound that ground measurements exceed only rarely, mostly with a low Sun.

Method and checks

Declination and equation of time: Fourier series of Spencer (1971), in the form published by National Oceanic and Atmospheric Administration, the US agency for the ocean and the atmosphere. Its GML laboratory publishes the solar position equations used here. in General Solar Position Calculations. Sunrise and sunset: zenith angle of 90.833°, which accounts for refraction and the radius of the solar disc, as in that document.

Checked against Free Python library for photovoltaic modeling, which includes several solar position algorithms. 0.15.2 (NREL Solar Position Algorithm of NREL, the US National Renewable Energy Laboratory (Reda and Andreas, 2004): the reference algorithm for solar position. algorithm) for Paris and Sydney on the 2026 equinoxes and solstices: sunrise, noon and sunset within 3 minutes.

Gsc = 1361 W/m² (Kopp and Lean, 2011); E0 and cos θz: standard textbook formulas (Duffie and Beckman, 2013). Legal times: Internet Assigned Numbers Authority: the body that maintains the world database of time zones and their clock changes, used by browsers. zones Europe/Paris and Australia/Sydney from the browser. Map: Natural Earth 1:110m land (public domain).

The exact formulas of the code, in plain text

n: day of the year (1 to 365); h: decimal UTC hour; t: UTC minutes since midnight
φ: latitude; λ: longitude, positive to the east (degrees)

γ   = 2π/365 · (n − 1 + (h − 12)/24)
δ   = 0.006918 − 0.399912 cos γ + 0.070257 sin γ − 0.006758 cos 2γ + 0.000907 sin 2γ − 0.002697 cos 3γ + 0.00148 sin 3γ   [rad]
EoT = 229.18 · (0.000075 + 0.001868 cos γ − 0.032077 sin γ − 0.014615 cos 2γ − 0.040849 sin 2γ)   [min]
H   = (t + EoT + 4·λ)/4 − 180   [°, hour angle]
cos θz = sin φ sin δ + cos φ cos δ cos H
cos H0 = cos(90.833°) / (cos φ cos δ) − tan φ tan δ
sunrise, sunset [min UTC] = 720 − 4·λ − EoT ∓ 4·H0   (H0 in degrees)
E0  = 1 + 0.033 cos(2π n/365)
G0  = 1361 · E0 · max(0, cos θz)   [W/m²]

Source: editorial/charte/sun-seasons.js (functions spencer, cosz, e0, g0), identical to the NOAA equations.
