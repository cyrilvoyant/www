Teaching

Five courses, open to all under CC BY 4.0, from health studies to energy. The same habit runs through them: start from the signal or the data, compare with a simple reference, measure what a model adds.

Health studies (PASS, the French first-year health program): biostatistics, then physics. B.U.T. (bachelor universitaire de technologie, a three-year technology degree) in biological engineering: food heat transfer (1st year), then industrial physics (2nd year). Engineer or researcher in energy: solar forecasting.

Open courses

Most courses are in French; the English guide to the open courses describes each one, chapter by chapter.

Forecasting the intermittent solar resource

Mines Paris – PSL engineering curriculum, applied research project (teaching unit UE14) · 59 slides · English and French · CC BY 4.0

One hour ahead, on the 2007 test year in Ajaccio, the daytime nRMSE (the root of the mean squared error, divided by mean irradiance) is 0.220 for smart persistence (the forecast keeps the present gap to clear sky), 0.213 for an AR(2) (an autoregressive model: a combination of the last two values) and 0.205 for an ELM (Extreme Learning Machine: a small neural network whose output weights alone are estimated). On ten years of real measurements, the course teaches how to forecast irradiance (the solar power received per square meter, in W/m²) and how to tell whether a gain justifies the complexity.

  • The solar signal: geometry, clear sky (the irradiance you would get without clouds) and the difference to the clear-sky trend (measured irradiance minus clear-sky irradiance; denoted CSI in the course), what can and cannot be predicted.
  • The data: ten years of hourly measurements, quality control, day and night.
  • Autoregressive models (the forecast is a combination of the last values) fitted by least squares (minimizing the sum of squared errors), the model order (the number of past values used), the sliding window (the model is re-estimated on the most recent data).
  • The references to beat: simple persistence (the last measured value is copied as is), clear sky, smart persistence (the present gap to clear sky is kept).
  • Evaluation: normalized mean bias (nMBE), mean absolute error (MAE), root mean squared error (RMSE) and its relative version (nRMSE), skill score SS (the gain over smart persistence), NICE (the error divided by that of simple persistence); test on the year 2007, daytime only, then day and night.
  • Extreme Learning Machines (ELM): principle, number of neurons, the risk of overfitting (a model so flexible that it follows the noise of the estimation data instead of the signal).
  • Online learning (the model updates as new measurements arrive) and frugality: how much model is really needed?

Try it: Forecast comparison · CLIPER and BLEND · NICE · sCV and F · Which forecast for which signal? · Sun, seasons and timestamps

Biostatistics for health studies

First-year health studies (PASS, UE8) · 3 chapters, 246 slides in French · summaries, experiments and FAQ in English · CC BY 4.0

With a sensitivity (the share of sick people the test detects) and a specificity (the share of healthy people it clears) of 99%, a positive test points to a sick person only about one time in eleven if the prevalence (the share of sick people in the population) is 1/1000. The course gives the tools to read a study, interpret a test and decide with data.

  • Why statistics in health: variability, therapeutic adjustment (adapting the dose to each patient), correlation and causation, hidden variables (unmeasured factors that create an apparent link).
  • Probabilities: random experiments, events, counting, conditional probabilities (the probability of A given B), the Bayesian approach (revising a probability in the light of a result).
  • Diagnostic tests: contingency tables (the counts of two variables crossed), sensitivity, specificity, predictive values (the probability of being sick, or not, given the test result), ROC curves (sensitivity against 1 − specificity as the test threshold varies).
  • Descriptive statistics: measurement scales, graphs, central tendency (mean, median), dispersion (standard deviation), skewness (the shape of the distribution).
  • The normal distribution (the bell curve): standardization (bringing the mean to 0 and the standard deviation to 1), the z table (the probabilities of the standard normal distribution), intervals.
  • Hypothesis tests: null (no effect) and alternative hypotheses, risks of error, comparison of means.
  • Pearson correlation (r, between −1 and 1: the strength of the linear link), linear regression (the line that predicts y from x), coefficient of determination (R², the share of variation explained) and significance.
  • χ² (chi-squared, which compares observed counts with expected counts) goodness-of-fit and independence tests, with exercises.

Try it: ROC curve · Normal distribution · Student t-test · Influential point in regression · Significance and R² · χ² test · Regression intervals

Physics for health studies: electromagnetic waves, X-rays and imaging (in French)

First-year health studies (PASS), physical bases of medical imaging · 110-slide lecture and 2 corrected exercise sessions · in French · CC BY 4.0

Photon energy (a photon is the elementary grain of light or X-rays) makes the contrast of a radiograph: at low energy, bone absorbs far more than soft tissue. The course gives the physical bases of medical imaging, from the wave to the CT scanner.

  • The nature of electromagnetic waves: speed, sinusoidal wave, wave vector (it gives the direction of propagation and the wavelength).
  • Wave-particle duality: photon energy E = hν (E the energy, h the Planck constant, ν the frequency), the electronvolt (eV, the energy unit for photons: 1 eV = 1.6·10⁻¹⁹ J).
  • The electromagnetic spectrum and its energy ranges.
  • The energy levels of the atom and the case of hydrogen.
  • Absorption, emission, fluorescence (the atom re-emits a photon), Auger effect (the atom ejects an electron instead of a photon), spectroscopy; an application: pulse oximetry (measuring blood oxygen from the absorption of light).
  • X-rays: the Coolidge tube (the tube that produces them), bremsstrahlung (continuous spectrum) and fluorescence radiation (lines), spectrum, radiation protection.
  • Photon-matter interactions: photoelectric effect (the photon is absorbed), Compton effect (the photon is scattered while giving up part of its energy), pair production (the photon becomes an electron and a positron), attenuation (the loss of photons from the beam) and half-value layer (the thickness that halves the beam).
  • Imaging: contrast, conventional radiography, CT scanning and windowing (the choice of the range of gray levels displayed).
  • Two exercise sessions corrected step by step (18 exercises).

Try it: Electromagnetic wave · Attenuation and half-value layer · Compton scattering

Industrial physics: thermodynamics, water vapor and refrigeration (in French)

B.U.T. biological engineering, food science and biotechnology, 2nd year · 5 chapters, 139 slides and handouts · in French · CC BY 4.0

Between −10 °C and +40 °C, a refrigeration machine cannot exceed a COP (coefficient of performance) of 5.26, or 5.26 J of cooling per joule of work. The course leads to this calculation: the thermodynamics of a food-industry technician, up to how the machine works.

  • Quantities: pressure, temperature, work and heat; sensible heat (it changes the temperature) and latent heat (it changes the state).
  • Changes of state: evaporation, boiling, triple point (where solid, liquid and vapor coexist), condensation.
  • Ideal and real gases; enthalpy (the energy of one kilogram of fluid in a machine balance) and entropy (whose creation measures irreversibility).
  • Clapeyron (pressure-volume), entropy (temperature-entropy) and Mollier (pressure-enthalpy in refrigeration) diagrams; reading the P-h diagram of R134a (a refrigerant); linear interpolation (estimating a value between two lines of a table).
  • Water vapor: expansion and compression, altitude, condensation, saturation vapor pressure (the maximum vapor pressure at a given temperature), relative humidity (the vapor present divided by that maximum).
  • First law (energy conservation) and second law (entropy creation is positive or zero), heat engines, engine cycles and steam cycles.
  • The four-step refrigeration cycle on the Mollier diagram, its COP, industrial refrigeration.
  • Refrigerants and their regulation (CFC and HCFC: chlorofluorocarbons and hydrochlorofluorocarbons).
  • Five chapters of written handouts come with the slides.

Try it: Heat engine and Carnot · Refrigeration, COP and Carnot · Freezing: energy and power

Heat transfer for food engineering (in French)

B.U.T. biological engineering, food science and biotechnology, 1st year · 24 slides · in French · CC BY 4.0

Blowing harder no longer cools a thick food: the Biot number (the ratio of a food's internal resistance to its surface resistance) tells whether the limit is at the surface or in the core. The course explains how heat moves through a food, and what it takes to cool or freeze it.

  • The three modes of exchange: conduction (from neighbour to neighbour), convection (carried by a moving fluid), radiation (by electromagnetic waves).
  • Basic concepts: isothermal surfaces (at the same temperature), heat flow (thermal power, in W) and heat flux density (the flow per square meter, in W/m²).
  • Conduction: Fourier's law (the flow is proportional to the temperature difference), conduction thermal resistance (what slows the passage of heat).
  • Free convection (natural motion of the fluid) and forced convection (fan, pump): heat transfer coefficient h (flow per square meter and per degree of difference), convection resistance.
  • The Biot number: comparing internal and surface resistances.
  • Thermal properties of fresh and frozen foods: specific heat capacity (the energy to warm one kilogram by one degree), initial freezing temperature.
  • The refrigeration power (the energy to remove per second) of cooling and freezing, the amount of water to freeze.
  • Energy balance: core temperature (conduction) and surface temperature (convection) over time.
