What the course contains

Mines Paris – PSL engineering curriculum, applied research project UE14 · 59 slides · English and French · CC BY 4.0

How to forecast solar irradiance one hour ahead, on ten years of real measurements from Ajaccio, adding complexity only where it pays.

  • The solar signal: geometry, clear sky and the clear-sky index (an additive deviation), what can and cannot be predicted.
  • The data: ten years of hourly measurements, quality control, day and night.
  • The references to beat: simple persistence, smart persistence, climatology.
  • Autoregressive models fitted by least squares, the model order, the sliding window.
  • Extreme Learning Machines: principle, number of neurons, the risk of overfitting.
  • Evaluation: nRMSE, nMAE, skill, NICE; test on the year 2007, daytime and day and night.
  • Online learning and frugality: how much model is really needed?

In brief

  • A 59-slide course on forecasting solar irradiance one hour ahead, taught in the UE14 applied research project of the Mines Paris – PSL engineering curriculum, built on ten years of hourly measurements from Ajaccio (Corsica).
  • It goes from the solar signal and the clear-sky index to autoregressive models fitted by least squares, then to Extreme Learning Machines, online learning and frugality, every model judged against persistence; on the 2007 test year, the daytime nRMSE is 22.0 % for smart persistence, 21.3 % for AR(2) and 20.5 % for an ELM.

1 · Why forecast solar power?

5 Why forecast? Grid and solar production

Text of the slide

The grid operator balances PV, storage, other plants and consumers.

Solar output varies with clouds. To integrate it into the grid, it must be anticipated.

Grid stability

Anticipate production drops to keep the frequency stable.

Supply / demand balance

Adjust the other plants (hydro, pumped storage*, gas…).

* Pumped-storage hydropower: a reversible dam that stores energy by pumping water uphill.

Storage

Charge or discharge batteries at the right time.

Economic value

Sell production at the best price and cut operating costs.

6 Irradiance, irradiation and photovoltaic output

Text of the slide

Irradiance (W/m²)

Power received per m² at a given instant. Example: 1,000 W/m² in full sun at noon.

From irradiance to electrical power

P ≈ η · S · G

Output is proportional to irradiance: forecasting GHI is forecasting production.

Irradiation (Wh/m²)

Energy received per m² over a duration. Over one hour: hourly irradiation in Wh/m² = mean irradiance in W/m².

η: module efficiency (≈ 20 %), S: area (m²), G: irradiance on the module (W/m²).

Example: 10 m² under 800 W/m² → P ≈ 0.20 × 10 × 800 = 1.6 kW.

Our data

Hourly GHI in Wh/m². Météo-France files are in J/cm²: 1 J/cm² = 2.78 Wh/m², so Wh/m² = J/cm² / 0.36.

2 · Solar radiation

8 Components of global radiation

Text of the slide

θz: zenith angle (between the Sun and the vertical). GHI = Global Horizontal Irradiance.

Direct (DNI)

Received straight from the Sun, measured on a surface perpendicular to the rays (direct normal). Dominant under clear sky.

Diffuse (DHI)

Scattered by air, aerosols and clouds. Dominant under overcast sky.

Reflected (albedo)

Sent back by the ground. Counts only for a tilted surface, not for GHI.

9 Crossing the atmosphere

Text of the slide

Through the atmosphere, radiation is attenuated by four mechanisms.

Absorption · Rayleigh scattering

Water vapour, ozone, CO₂: part of the energy is absorbed.

By air molecules: it makes the sky blue.

Mie scattering · Clouds

By aerosols (dust, sea salt, pollution): depends on turbidity.

Reflection, scattering and absorption: main cause of intermittency.

Ajaccio, 2007: a clear day and an overcast day.

Under overcast sky, GHI drops here to about 20 % of its clear-sky value.

10 The position of the Sun

Text of the slide

Solar elevation over the day at Ajaccio, by season.

Zenith angle θz: between the Sun and the vertical. The larger it is, the thicker the atmosphere crossed.

Declination δ: angle between the Sun's direction and the equatorial plane; from −23.45° to +23.45° over the year.

Hour angle ω: 15° per hour, zero at solar noon. Latitude φ: 41.9° at Ajaccio.

cos θz = sin φ · sin δ + cos φ · cos δ · cos ω

G0 = I0 · (d0/d)2 · cos θz

G0: extraterrestrial radiation; I0 ≈ 1361 W/m² (solar constant; 1367 in older codes).

The Earth–Sun distance d varies by ±1.7 %, so (d0/d)2 varies by ±3.3 %.

11 The double seasonality of GHI

Text of the slide

Hourly GHI in 2007: hours of the day × days of the year (Ajaccio).

Daily seasonality

Zero at night, maximum around solar noon: a 24-h cycle well described by the clear-sky model.

Annual seasonality

The amplitude of the daily cycle changes with the season (declination, day length): a 365-day cycle.

Raw GHI is not stationary: its mean depends on the hour and the month. Both cycles must be removed before modeling.

3 · Time series and forecasting

12 What is a time series?

Text of the slide

A sequence of measurements y1, y2, …, yN of one quantity, taken at a constant time step Δt. Here Δt = 1 h.

Three properties

Regularity: measurements are equally spaced.

Dependence: the past informs the future.

Randomness: each value has an unpredictable part.

What we want for modeling

Stationarity: stable mean and variance over time.

Complete series: no missing values.

Ajaccio: one week of hourly GHI.

Raw GHI is not stationary (day/night and seasonal cycles): that is the whole point of chapter 4.

13 Components of a time series

Text of the slide

Trend Tt

Slow long-term change: increasing, decreasing or none.

Cycle Ct

Long, not strictly periodic oscillations (climate phenomena).

For GHI: seasonality dominates, with two cycles (daily and annual).

Seasonality St

Regular, repeatable cycles (day, year). Very strong for GHI.

Residual Rt

Unexplained random part. Ideally white noise (independent values, zero mean).

14 Decomposing a time series

Text of the slide

Additive model

yt = Tt + St + Ct + Rt

When the amplitude of fluctuations stays constant.

Multiplicative model

yt = Tt × St × Ct × Rt

When the amplitude grows with the level (GHI: small in winter, large in summer).

Why decompose?

Remove trend and seasonality (the part predictable without effort);

keep a residual that is easier to model;

check that this residual looks like white noise.

Link with what follows: subtracting clear-sky GHI, CSIt = GHIt − GHIcst, applies the additive model: both cycles go at once. The ratio (multiplicative model) also exists: chapter 4.

15 Horizon, time step and types of forecast

Text of the slide

Usual methods by horizon (orders of magnitude).

Time step Δt = 1 h; horizon h in steps: h = 1 → 1 h, h = 3 → 3 h.

Direct forecast: one model trained for each horizon h.

Recursive forecast: forecast t + 1, then feed that forecast back to forecast t + 2, and so on.

In this course: 1-h horizon, direct forecast.

16 Forecasting: a predictable part and an error

Text of the slide

yt+1 = f(yt, yt−1, …, yt−p+1) + εt+1

The predictable part

f sums up what the past tells us about the future. The forecast is ŷt+1 = f(yt, …, yt−p+1).

The framework suits GHI: regular measurements and strong hour-to-hour dependence (cloud inertia). Further reading: appendix B.

The error

εt+1 = yt+1 − ŷt+1 is the unpredictable part. If the model is good, ε looks like white noise.

The goal

Choose f so that errors are as small as possible in mean square.

17 A first example: the AR(1) model, with numbers

Text of the slide

yt+1 = w · yt + εt+1

yt = CSIt − μ: gap of the CSI to its mean; w: weight of the last gap.

|w| < 1: the series always returns to its mean.

ŷt+h = wh · yt

Ajaccio: μ ≈ −33 Wh/m² and w ≈ 0.84. A cloud: CSIt = −300 Wh/m², so yt = −267.

ŷt+1 = 0.84 × (−267) = −224: forecast CSI = −257 Wh/m²; then −221 and −191 at 2 h and 3 h.

The forecast CSI returns to its mean as h grows.

4 · Clear sky and clear-sky index

18 The clear-sky model

Text of the slide

What is it?

The GHI one would measure without clouds, given the Sun's position and a clear atmosphere.

Inputs

site position (latitude, longitude, altitude);

Sun position (θz);

water vapour and aerosols (AOD: aerosol optical depth).

Role: GHIcst is the reference used to remove the day/night and seasonal cycles.

Beer–Lambert law: intensity decays exponentially with the path length crossed.

19 The Solis model

Text of the slide

Solis (Ineichen, 2008) is a simplified version of a radiative-transfer model. It starts from the Beer–Lambert law:

GHIcs ≈ I0 · exp(−τ / cos θz) · cos θz

τ: optical depth (air, water vapour, aerosols);

Solis adjusts τ and the exponent for water vapour, aerosols and altitude.

Settings: rural zone, water column 3.79 cm, AOD 0.16, I0 = 1367 W/m².

The hourly value is the mean of the past hour: the Sun is computed at mid-hour, ω = (i − 12.5) × 15°.

Mean clear-sky error at Ajaccio: ≈ 32 Wh/m².

Ajaccio, 2007: Solis and measurement under clear sky.

20 Stationarizing: the clear-sky index (CSI)

Text of the slide

CSIt = GHIt − GHIcst

≈ 0 under clear sky, and 0 at night;

negative under clouds (down to −GHIcs);

mean μ ≈ −33 Wh/m²: we center it, yt = CSIt − μ.

The procedure

  • compute GHIcs, then the CSI;
  • center: yt = CSIt − μ;
  • forecast ŷt+h;
  • rebuild GHIfct+h = ŷt+h + μ + GHIcst+h (0 at night, never negative).

Ajaccio, 2007: top, GHI; bottom, CSI. The daily cycle has gone from the CSI.

21 Two ways to stationarize

Text of the slide

The Solis model removes the cycles in two ways:

Difference (additive model), this course

CSIt = GHIt − GHIcst, in Wh/m²;

no division: valid day and night (at night, 0 − 0 = 0);

all hours are kept, no filtering.

For hourly data with nights, the ratio is poorly suited: we use the difference. The ratio is also very sensitive to small timestamp offsets and to the filtering thresholds chosen (Voyant et al., 2025a; 2026).

Ratio (multiplicative model)

kt = GHIt / GHIcst, unitless (≈ 1 under clear sky); widely used in the literature;

at night: 0 / 0, undefined; at sunrise and sunset, GHIcs ≈ 0: values explode;

requires filtering on solar elevation (for instance keep h > 10°).

22 Is the CSI stationary?

Text of the slide

Mean ± standard deviation by month, daytime hours, Ajaccio 1998–2007.

The CSI is approximately stationary: much more than GHI, though not perfectly. Formal definition: appendix A.

GHI: the mean ranges from 163 to 521 Wh/m² depending on the month.

CSI: the mean stays between −105 and −43 Wh/m², close to 0.

The CSI standard deviation still varies (97 to 185 Wh/m²): more clouds in spring.

23 Site variability: the sCV

Text of the slide

How far does GHI depart, on average, from clear sky? We measure the gap εt = GHIt − GHIcst (the CSI):

sCV = (√2 / (1 − d)) · √[ Σt (GHIt − GHIcst)2 / Σt (GHIcst)2 ]

Numerator: typical size of the gaps to clear sky (the clouds).

Denominator: typical size of clear-sky GHI.

√2 / (1 − d) scales the worst case to 1: clear sky, then very overcast, every other hour (d = 0.2: even under overcast sky, at least 20 % of clear sky remains).

sCV = 0: always clear; sCV = 1: maximum variability. It does not depend on the horizon: it is a property of the site.

Ajaccio 1998–2007: sCV and F at 1 h, month by month (Voyant et al., 2026).

Ajaccio: sCV = 0.52 over 1998–2007.

Winter: 0.8 (changeable sky); July: 0.3 (often clear sky).

24 Forecastability: F depends on the horizon

Text of the slide

A variable series is not necessarily unpredictable: if the gaps to clear sky last, they can be anticipated.

ρ(k): correlation between the clear-sky gap now, εt, and k hours later, εt+k (the memory of clouds).

ρmax(h): the strongest of these correlations for k between h and 12 h: the memory left when forecasting at horizon h.

F(h) = 1 − sCV · (1 − ρmax(h))

perfect memory (ρmax = 1): F = 1, everything is predictable;

no memory (ρmax = 0): F = 1 − sCV.

Ajaccio 1998–2007: F and remaining memory by horizon (Voyant et al., 2026).

F = 0.92 at 1 h, 0.74 at 3 h, 0.53 at 6 h.

The atmosphere is chaotic: cloud memory fades within a few hours. sCV does not move.

5 · AR model and least squares

26 From the AR model to linear regression

Text of the slide

An AR(p) model forecasts the next value from the last p values:

yt+1 = w1 yt + w2 yt−1 + … + wp yt−p+1 + εt+1

Matrix form

y = X w + ε

It is a multiple linear regression. Since y is centred, no intercept is needed.

y (N × 1): the values to forecast;

X (N × p): one row per example, with the p lags;

w (p × 1): the coefficients to estimate;

ε (N × 1): the errors.

27 Building X and y: a worked example

Text of the slide

Five centred CSI values (Wh/m²): y1 = −10; y2 = −25; y3 = −240; y4 = −180; y5 = −30. Take p = 2.

Rowytyt−1Target yt+1
t = 2−25−10−240
t = 3−240−25−180
t = 4−180−240−30

The two middle columns form X (3 × 2), the last one forms y (3 × 1).

Each row shifts the previous one by one step.

In practice: all hours are kept, nights included (at night, y = −μ ≈ 33). At Ajaccio: 8 years, about 70,000 rows for training.

28 Ordinary least squares (OLS)

Text of the slide

We look for the w that minimizes the sum of squared errors:

J(w) = ‖y − Xw‖2 = Σi (yi − xiT w)2

At the minimum, the derivative is zero:

−2 XT(y − Xw) = 0 ⟹ XTX w = XTy

Conditions: many more examples than coefficients (N ≫ p);

columns of X not proportional to each other.

ŵ = (XTX)−1 XTy

Exact solution, in one step: no iteration, a single minimum.

Ajaccio, AR(2): ŵ = (0.97; −0.15), computed in 5 ms.

The last gap yt weighs by far the most.

29 Geometric interpretation

Text of the slide

Xŵ is the point of the “plane of the columns of X” closest to y: its orthogonal projection.

The error ε = y − Xŵ is perpendicular to this plane.

Pythagoras: ‖y‖2 = ‖ŷ‖2 + ‖ε‖2. This is the basis of the R2 coefficient.

OLS does not “guess” anything: it projects the measurements onto what the inputs can explain.

31 Choosing p (1/3): autocorrelation (ACF)

Text of the slide

ACF of the centred CSI, Ajaccio 1998–2005, all hours.

ACF(k): correlation between yt and yt−k, the value k hours earlier.

Here ACF(1) = 0.84, ACF(2) = 0.67, ACF(3) = 0.50: it decays slowly.

But this correlation propagates step by step: if yt depends on yt−1, which depends on yt−2, then yt and yt−2 are correlated even without a direct link.

The ACF therefore does not tell how many lags are really useful. The PACF is needed.

32 Choosing p (2/3): partial autocorrelation (PACF)

Text of the slide

PACF of the centred CSI, Ajaccio 1998–2005, all hours.

PACF(k): once yt−1, …, yt−k+1 are known, what does yt−k add? It is the last coefficient wk of an AR(k).

A bar outside the blue band: this lag brings direct information.

For a true AR(p), the PACF vanishes after lag p.

Here PACF(1) = 0.84, PACF(2) = −0.15, then values below 0.08: the PACF suggests p ≈ 2.

But with N ≈ 70,000 points, the ±0.007 threshold is tiny: almost everything is “significant”. Significant ≠ useful.

33 Choosing p (3/3): filter or wrapper approach

Text of the slide

Filter

look at the data alone, before any model: PACF, AIC / BIC criteria (appendix C);

fast, but indirect.

Wrapper

train an AR(p) for p = 1 to 12 on 1998–2005;

measure the forecast error on validation (2006);

driven by the real objective, but more costly.

Wrapper approach: validation RMSE (2006, daytime hours) versus p.

p = 1: 78.0; p = 2: 77.7; p = 12: 77.3 Wh/m². Parsimony: the smallest p within 0.5 % of the best, i.e. p = 2.

6 · Evaluation, ELM and online learning

35 Reference models

Text of the slide

Every model must be compared with a simple reference. If it does not do better, it adds nothing.

Simple persistence

“It will be as it is now.”

GHIfct+1 = GHIt

Ignores the Sun's course.

Clear sky

“It will be sunny.”

GHIfct+1 = GHIcst+1

Ignores clouds.

Smart persistence

“The sky will stay the same.”

GHIfct+1 = GHIt − GHIcst + GHIcst+1

Keeps the same gap to clear sky.

Smart persistence = AR(1) with w = 1: the reference of the skill score SS.

Two rules, for every model: 1) at night (Sun below the horizon, θz > 90°), forecast = 0; 2) a negative forecast is replaced by 0.

36 Measuring the error

Text of the slide

et = GHIfct − GHIt (forecast error)

nMBE = [(1/N) Σ et] / mean(GHIt)

MAE = (1/N) Σ |et|

RMSE = √[(1/N) Σ et2] ; nRMSE = RMSE / mean(GHIt)

Essential check: if |nMBE| > 1 %, there is probably a problem, or a very poor model. The bias can be removed, provided this is tested; we do not do it here.

Errors are computed on the rebuilt GHI, over the test year (2007): first on daytime hours, then day + night. Always say which!

nMBE (bias): > 0 if the model over-estimates on average, < 0 if it under-estimates. Errors of opposite sign cancel: it is not a score for ranking models.

MAE: mean error, in Wh/m², easy to interpret.

RMSE: penalizes large errors more (sudden cloud). Always ≥ MAE.

nRMSE: RMSE as % of mean GHI, to compare sites.

37 Comparing with persistence: SS and NICEΣ

Text of the slide

SS = 1 − RMSEmodel / RMSEsmart persistence

Skill score: > 0 if smart persistence is beaten, < 0 otherwise.

Looks only at the RMSE.

Two different references: SS compares with smart persistence, NICEΣ with simple persistence (Voyant et al., 2025b).

NICEk = Lk(model) / Lk(simple persistence)

Lk = ((1/N) Σ |et|k)1/k ; NICEΣ = (NICE1 + NICE2 + NICE3) / 3

et = GHIfct − GHIt: the forecast error (previous slide).

k = 1: average errors (MAE); k = 2: RMSE; k = 3: large errors;

NICEΣ < 1: the model beats simple persistence on all three norms at once.

40 The bias (nMBE): effect of the two rules

Text of the slide

41 Why does persistence go from 0 to −1 %?

Text of the slide

second-to-last · daytime hour · last · daytime hour · 150 · 43 · Measurement · … · 150 · Persistence, no rules · … · 150

With rule 1 (night)

Values in Wh/m²; 43 Wh/m² = 2007 mean of the last daytime hour.

A single hour per day, but it weighs heavily: the day + night mean is only 186 Wh/m², because night hours count as zero.

first · night hour

No rules: persistence copies the previous hour's measurement. Over the year, each measurement is used once as measurement and once as forecast: the two sums are equal, nMBE = 0.

With rule 1 (night): at the first night hour the Sun is below the horizon, so the forecast 43 is replaced by 0. Every day, a forecast of about 43 Wh/m² disappears.

Over the year: 365 × 43 ≈ 15,642 Wh/m², plus 1,008 Wh/m² of small values measured at night: 16,650 Wh/m² are missing.

Relative to the GHI measured over the year (1,630,161 Wh/m²): 1.02 %, hence a day + night nMBE of −1.02 %.

0 · 43 · 0

The rule is physically right (no Sun at night), but it changes the bias.

43 Limits of the AR model

Text of the slide

AR(p) assumes a linear relation between the CSI and its lags. Often enough at short horizons, but not always.

Difficult situations

fast clear / overcast transitions (cloud fronts);

cloud-edge effects (GHI > GHIcs);

very variable sky (cumulus);

nonlinear links between lags.

Under-fitting

A linear model cannot capture a curved relation, even with many lags.

Saturation

Beyond some p, adding lags no longer improves anything (slide “Choosing p”).

Idea: transform the inputs nonlinearly, then keep least squares. That is the ELM.

44 Extreme Learning Machine (ELM): the principle

Text of the slide

One-hidden-layer ELM (Huang et al., 2006).

A neural network with a single hidden layer.

Input weights Win and biases b are drawn at random, then frozen.

Each neuron applies a nonlinear function g (tanh, sigmoid…).

Only the output weights β are learnt, by least squares.

No gradient back-propagation: training is done in a single solve.

45 ELM: the formulation

Text of the slide

The p lags of each row go through the hidden layer:

H = g(X Win + b) (N × L)

ModelLeast-squares solutionColumns used
ARy = Xw + εŵ = (XTX)−1XTylags of the centred CSI
ELMy = Hβ + εβ̂ = (HTH + αI)−1HTyoutputs of the L neurons

Same equation, only the columns change. The small term αI (ridge regression) stabilizes the solve when L is large; at Ajaccio, α = 10−3.

46 ELM: choosing the number of neurons L

Text of the slide

Validation RMSE (2006, daytime hours) versus L, for p = 2.

L = 5: 85.2 Wh/m² → under-fitting.

L = 50: 75.9 Wh/m².

Beyond, no further gain: 75.8 at L = 200, 75.9 at L = 500 (risk of over-fitting).

As for p: test a few values (5 to 500) on validation and keep the smallest near-optimal one. Here L = 50.

48 Giving the model the time: sin / cos encoding

Text of the slide

Idea: add calendar variables known in advance to the lags: the hour h and the day of year j.

Problem: coded as a number, 23:00 is “far” from 00:00, although they are neighbouring hours.

Solution: place the hour on a circle, with two variables:

(sin(2π h / 24); cos(2π h / 24)) and (sin(2π j / 365); cos(2π j / 365))

4 more columns in X. The model then “knows” the hour and the season (Voyant et al., 2025a).

50 AR or ELM? The verdict

Text of the slide

ELM strengths

fast training, a single solve;

captures nonlinear relations;

same method (OLS) as AR.

About 100 times more coefficients (learnt + random) for 4 more points of skill score. Is the gain worth the complexity?

ELM limits

L must be chosen;

result varies with the draw;

less interpretable than AR;

over-fits if L grows large;

needs a lot of data.

At Ajaccio, 1 h ahead

AR(2): 2 coefficients; nRMSE 21.3 %.

ELM: 51 learnt + 150 random; nRMSE 20.5 %.

Skill score SS: +0.03 (AR) versus +0.07 (ELM).

51 Online learning (1/2): the sliding window

Text of the slide

Forecast · Observe · hour t

hour t + 1

blue: the W training hours; pink: the hour to forecast

Matrix X always keeps W rows: the last W hours.

Every hour, the newest row is added and the oldest removed, then ridge is re-estimated:

ŵ = (XTX + αI)−1XTy

Update

Trick: no need to recompute everything. XTX ← XTX + xnewxnewT − xoldxoldT.

Same for the ELM, with H instead of X.

Update: 11 µs for AR, 38 µs for the ELM, per hour.

Useful when the system changes (drifting sensor, soiled panel, new site): the model forgets the distant past.

Appendices

57 Appendix A, Wide-sense stationarity

Text of the slide

A process (zt) is wide-sense stationary if:

E[zt] = μ (constant) and Cov(zt, zt+k) = γ(k) (depends only on the lag k)

In practice

the mean is constant over time;

the variance is finite and constant;

the dependence between two instants depends only on their gap.

Simple check: plot the mean and standard deviation month by month (slide “Is the CSI stationary?”). Statistical tests exist but are not needed here.

58 Appendix B, The best possible forecast

Text of the slide

ŷt+h = E[yt+h | yt, yt−1, …, yt−p+1]

Among all forecasts built from the past, the one that minimizes the mean squared error is the conditional expectation: the mean of possible futures, given the past.

We then write yt+h = ŷt+h + εt+h: predictable part + error, with a zero-mean error.

An AR(p) model is a linear approximation of this expectation; the ELM, a nonlinear one.

If the model errors form white noise (non-significant error ACF), no usable information is left in the past.

59 Appendix C, Choosing p with AIC or BIC

Text of the slide

AIC ≈ N · ln(σ̂2) + 2(p+1)

BIC ≈ N · ln(σ̂2) + (p+1) · ln N

σ̂2: variance of the training errors;

keep the p that gives the smallest criterion.

The first term falls as the model fits better; the second penalizes each added coefficient.

BIC penalizes more than AIC: it picks smaller models.

Advantage: no validation period needed. Drawback: assumes Gaussian errors.
