Chapter 1: probability, conditional probability, contingency tables and ROC curves

Why health needs statistics, probability vocabulary, counting, conditional probability and Bayes' theorem, contingency tables, sensitivity, specificity, ROC curves.

Slides (78, in French) · PDF in French (4.7 MB) · Questions

In brief

  • Chapter 1 of the biostatistics course, first year of health studies in France (PASS), teaching unit UE8.
  • What you should be able to do: compute a conditional probability, read a contingency table, go from sensitivity and specificity to predictive values using prevalence, read a ROC curve.
  • The key fact: with 99% sensitivity and specificity and a prevalence of 1 in 1000, a positive test points to a sick person only about one time in eleven.
  • To try below: the ROC curve, by moving the decision threshold.

The chapter in 10 points

Key point. With a sensitivity (the share of sick people detected) and a specificity (the share of healthy people cleared) of 99%, a positive test points to a sick person only about one time in eleven when the prevalence (the share of sick people in the population) is 1/1000: a Bayes calculation, not clinical guidance.

  1. Statistics defines what is normal, measures variability (between patients, between measurements) and adjusts treatments: the concentration of a drug varies widely from one patient to another for the same dose.
  2. Correlation is not causation: a hidden variable can create an apparent link, and the wording of a question changes the probability being sought (the two-children paradox).
  3. A probability is a model; the limiting frequency is its frequentist interpretation. P(A ∪ B) = P(A) + P(B) − P(A ∩ B) and P(Ā) = 1 − P(A), where A ∪ B means “A or B”, A ∩ B means “A and B”, and Ā is the complement of A.
  4. Under equiprobability, one counts: favorable cases over possible cases, with the tools of combinatorics.
  5. Conditional probability: P(A | B) = P(A ∩ B)/P(B), read “probability of A given B”; for independent events, P(A ∩ B) = P(A) P(B).
  6. Total probability and Bayes' theorem: P(D | +) = P(+ | D) P(D) / P(+), where D is the event “the person has the disease” and + the event “the test is positive”.
  7. Evaluating a test: among sick people, a positive result is a true positive (TP) and a negative one a false negative (FN); among healthy people, a negative result is a true negative (TN) and a positive one a false positive (FP). Sensitivity Se = TP/(TP + FN), specificity Sp = TN/(TN + FP).
  8. Positive and negative predictive values (PPV, NPV) depend on the prevalence p, the proportion of people with the disease: PPV = Se·p / [Se·p + (1 − Sp)(1 − p)], NPV = Sp(1 − p) / [Sp(1 − p) + (1 − Se) p].
  9. The ROC curve (receiver operating characteristic) plots the sensitivity Se against the false-positive rate 1 − Sp as the threshold varies. The area under this curve is the AUC (area under the curve). An AUC of 0.5 means that the score ranks no better than chance: a sick and a healthy person drawn at random are correctly ordered one time in two; it does not by itself describe the usefulness of a decision.
  10. The choice of threshold depends on the use, the prevalence and the consequences of errors. Maximizing Youden's index (Se + Sp − 1) and minimizing the distance to the corner (0, 1) are two different criteria.

I. Why statistics matters in health

Read

Medical progress rests on mathematics more often than one thinks. The CT scanner rebuilds an image of the body from X-ray projections taken at many angles, using the Radon transform; the 1979 Nobel Prize in Physiology or Medicine went to the engineer Godfrey N. Hounsfield and the physicist Allan M. Cormack for this work.

Statistics answers four questions that come back constantly in medical practice:

  • Defining what is normal. What is a “normal” height, weight, blood pressure or blood glucose? How do you tell a physiological variation from a sign of disease?
  • Evaluating examinations. How reliable is a test? What sensitivity (the share of sick people it detects) and specificity (the share of healthy people it clears) can be expected?
  • Assessing risk. What is the risk of a complication? What are the benefits and the adverse effects of a treatment? How likely is a rare but serious event?
  • Comparing treatments. Is treatment A really better than treatment B? How large is the effect, and does it matter clinically? What do randomized trials and meta-analyses (studies that pool the results of several trials) add?

Without statistics, medicine would remain empirical; with them, it becomes evidence-based medicine. The slides draw the chain from data to decision: medical observations (blood pressure, cholesterol, imaging, all affected by natural variability and measurement error) go through the statistical tool (describe with means and standard deviations, compare groups and hypotheses, estimate with a p-value or a 95% confidence interval, a range built so that 95% of such ranges contain the true value) and end in a health decision: adapting a treatment, evaluating a test, guiding public health.

Variability and dose adjustment. After the same standard dose, given as an 8-hour infusion, the plasma concentration of a drug differs widely from one patient to another. The effect of a drug depends on its pharmacokinetics (PK: what the body does to the drug, from absorption to elimination) and on its pharmacodynamics (PD: what the drug does to its molecular target, the organ and the whole body). A PK-PD model combines both, and the dose is adjusted to the patient.

The objectives of the course follow: understand the statistical methods used in medicine, interpret research results correctly, reason rigorously, and support decisions in practice. The course goes from probability theory to its applications in diagnosis (sensitivity, specificity, ROC curves), then to inferential statistics: hypotheses H₀ and H₁, for example to compare two treatments.

II. Probability

Read

Correlation is not causation. Three French regions (Brittany, Pays de la Loire and Normandy) hold 52% of the country's dairy cows. Set this map beside a map of alcohol consumption and you could “conclude” that drinking milk causes alcoholism: a deliberately absurd reading of a coincidence between two maps.

Hidden variables. According to INCa, the French National Cancer Institute, tobacco is a direct cause or a contributing factor of many cancers: more than 8 lung cancers in 10, nearly 70% of cancers of the upper aerodigestive tract (mouth, larynx, pharynx, esophagus) and 35% of bladder cancers. The slides then imagine a gene that would cause both addiction and uncontrolled cell growth: with such a hidden (confounding) variable, which acts on both, one could wrongly conclude that tobacco does not cause lung cancer. Probability can “explain everything”, and can also be used to “question everything”.

The two-children paradox (Martin Gardner, 1959): the wording changes the answer.

Mr. Jones has two children and the elder is a girl. Writing the elder first, the possible cases are GG and GB (G for girl, B for boy); one is favorable, so P(two girls) = 1/2.
Mr. Smith has two children and at least one is a boy. The possible cases are GB, BG and BB; one is favorable, so P(two boys) = 1/3.

A first look at the Bayesian approach. To adjust a dose, it combines the patient's own data (a few blood samples) with what is known of the population (the prior information): the dose becomes personal.

Vocabulary.

Random experiment
an experiment whose outcome cannot be predicted with certainty, even when it is repeated under the same conditions.
Sample space Ω
the set of all possible outcomes; an event is a subset of Ω.
Frequentist definition
the probability of an event is the limit of its relative frequency when the experiment is repeated many times.
Mutually exclusive (incompatible) events
events that cannot occur together: A ∩ B = ∅, and then P(A ∪ B) = P(A) + P(B).
Events that are not mutually exclusive
the intersection would be counted twice, so it is subtracted: P(A ∪ B) = P(A) + P(B) − P(A ∩ B).
Complement Ā
the event “A does not occur”: P(Ā) = 1 − P(A).
Probability
a number between 0 and 1 attached to each event, with P(Ω) = 1 and additivity for mutually exclusive events.
Equiprobability
when all outcomes are equally likely, P(A) = number of favorable cases / number of possible cases; probability then becomes counting.

III. Applications

Read

The exercises first practice the vocabulary (naming events, their union, intersection and complement), then counting.

Counting through the complement. Of 336 possible outfits, 3 × 3 × 5 = 45 are “blue” outfits. Rather than counting the others one by one, use the complement: 336 − 45 = 291 outfits are not.

A recessive genetic disease. An autosomal recessive disease affects 1 live birth in 2000. When both parents are heterozygous, each child has 1 chance in 4 of being affected. What is the probability that such a couple has two healthy children? A healthy child: 1 − 0.25 = 0.75. The two births are independent, so the probabilities multiply: 0.75 × 0.75 ≈ 0.56.

Vaccination. In a population, 45% are vaccinated against yellow fever (A), 60% against hepatitis B (B) and 30% against both. Probability of being vaccinated against at least one: P(A ∪ B) = 0.45 + 0.60 − 0.30 = 0.75. Probability of being vaccinated against neither: 1 − 0.75 = 0.25.

IV. Conditional probability

Read

“Given that” changes the reference population. The conditional probability of A given B counts A only among the cases where B occurred: P(A | B) = P(A ∩ B)/P(B), hence the multiplication rule P(A ∩ B) = P(A | B) P(B). Two events are independent when knowing one does not change the probability of the other: P(A | B) = P(A), that is P(A ∩ B) = P(A) P(B). For independent risks, the chance of escaping all of them is the product of the chances of escaping each one.

A useful theorem. Total probability splits an event along the two cases A and Ā: P(B) = P(B | A) P(A) + P(B | Ā) P(Ā). Bayes' theorem then reverses the conditioning: P(A | B) = P(B | A) P(A) / P(B). It goes from direct probabilities (cause → effect) to inverse probabilities (effect → cause), which is exactly the question of diagnosis.

Worked example: hospital-acquired infections. Earlier studies show that 25% of serious infections are nosocomial (acquired in hospital); 15% of the other serious infections lead to death, against 30% of the nosocomial ones. A patient dies after a serious infection: what is the probability that it was nosocomial?

P(death) = 0.25 × 0.30 + 0.75 × 0.15 = 0.075 + 0.1125 = 0.1875, so P(nosocomial | death) = 0.075/0.1875 = 0.40. Nosocomial infections are a quarter of serious infections but 40% of the deaths that follow them.

V. Contingency tables

Read

A diagnostic test is a Bayes problem. Its results are summarized in a contingency table that crosses the disease status (sick or healthy, according to a reference) with the test result. Four counts, defined once and for all:

True positive (TP)
a sick person whose test is positive.
False negative (FN)
a sick person whose test is negative: the disease is missed.
True negative (TN)
a healthy person whose test is negative.
False positive (FP)
a healthy person whose test is positive: a false alarm.

The prevalence p is the proportion of sick people in the population at a given time. The sensitivity Se = TP/(TP + FN) is the probability of a positive test in a sick person; the specificity Sp = TN/(TN + FP) is the probability of a negative test in a healthy person. Both describe the test. The positive predictive value PPV = TP/(TP + FP) is the probability of being sick when the test is positive, and the negative predictive value NPV = TN/(TN + FN) the probability of being healthy when it is negative. Both depend on the prevalence, which is why the same test does not mean the same thing in a screening campaign and in a specialist clinic.

Instead of the table, the slides also use a decision tree: a first branch for sick or healthy (prevalence), a second for the test result (Se, Sp). With whole numbers of people, it makes the key point of the chapter visible.

The key point, in natural frequencies. Se = Sp = 99%, prevalence 1/1000, 100 000 people.

PPV = 99/1098 ≈ 9%: about one positive in eleven is sick. NPV = 98 901/98 902, above 99.99%. In the HIV example of the slides, the exercise is to compute an NPV of 0.9999.

Reading the statement. In colon cancer screening with a prevalence of 10/1000, the test is positive in 90% of people with cancer and in 35% of people without: Se = 0.90 and Sp = 1 − 0.35 = 0.65. With Se = 98%, Sp = 90% and a prevalence of 10/1000: out of 1000 sick people, 980 test positive; out of 1000 people from the population, 990 are healthy and 10% of them, 99, are false positives.

Predictive value. A disease affects 20% of the population; the test has Se = 90% and Sp = 85% (it is positive in 15% of healthy people). PPV = 0.9 × 0.2 / (0.9 × 0.2 + 0.15 × 0.8) = 0.18/0.30 = 60%.

Cholesterol or apolipoprotein B? According to the WHO, cholesterol above 2 g/L or apolipoprotein B (ApoB) above 1.3 g/L are associated with cardiovascular risk. Among 66 subjects, 30 are at risk (R+) and 36 are not (R−). Three tests: A (cholesterol), B (ApoB), C (cholesterol and/or ApoB above threshold).

VI. ROC curves

Read

Many tests rest on a continuous measurement (a blood concentration, a score) and a threshold. Moving the threshold trades one error for the other: a lower threshold catches more sick people (Se rises) and raises more false alarms (Sp falls). The ROC curve plots every possible threshold as a point (1 − Sp, Se). The area under the curve (AUC) summarizes how well the measurement separates the two groups: 0.5 for chance, 1 for perfect separation. Of two curves, the one that lies above the other ranks better at every threshold; the slides compare three tests this way. You can move the threshold and the overlap of the two groups in the ROC curve experiment.

Worked example: BNP in acute heart failure. BNP (B-type natriuretic peptide) is secreted by the muscle cells of the left ventricle and relaxes the systemic and pulmonary arteries. A study determined BNP thresholds for diagnosing acute left heart failure in the emergency room. The slides read the published ROC curve: the AUC is significantly different from 0.5, so BNP carries diagnostic information; at 150 pg/mL, 1 − Sp = 20%, hence Sp = 80%; and 150 pg/mL is the threshold closest to the ideal corner (0, 1), the best Se/Sp compromise by that criterion. Other criteria, and the cost of each error, can lead to another threshold (see the questions below).

Going further: questions and answers

Why does prevalence change the predictive value?

With Se = Sp = 99% and a prevalence of 1/1000, among 1000 people one expects 0.99 true positives and 9.99 false positives, hence a PPV close to 9%. This illustrates Bayes' theorem; clinical management depends on context and protocol.

What does an AUC of 0.8 mean?

For a score oriented toward the presence of disease, AUC = P(score of a sick person > score of a healthy person) + 0.5 P(tie). An AUC of 0.8 describes this ranking, not the individual probability of being sick, nor the quality at a given threshold.

Does the best threshold always maximize Se + Sp?

No. Youden's index and the distance to the ideal corner optimize different criteria; neither automatically encodes the clinical cost of errors. A decision needs, among other things, the prevalence and the respective consequences of false positives and false negatives.

Why is the Bayesian approach used to adjust a dose?

It combines knowledge of the population (a prior distribution of the pharmacokinetic parameters) with the patient's own measurements: a few blood samples are enough for an individual estimate, which is refined with each new measurement.
