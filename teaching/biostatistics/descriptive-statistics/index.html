Chapter 2: descriptive statistics, the normal distribution and hypothesis tests

Measurement scales, graphs, central tendency and dispersion, the normal distribution and the z table, confidence intervals, hypotheses and the comparison of means.

Slides (99, in French) · PDF in French (8.2 MB) · Questions

In brief

  • Chapter 2 of the biostatistics course, first year of health studies in France (PASS), teaching unit UE8.
  • What you should be able to do: compute a mean, a variance and a standard deviation, standardize a value, give a 95% confidence interval for a mean, read a p-value, choose between a paired test and a test on two independent samples.
  • The key fact: a p-value of 0.03 does not mean that the tested hypothesis has a 3% chance of being true.
  • To try below: the normal distribution, and reading a difference with its uncertainty.

The chapter in 10 points

Key point. A p-value of 0.03 (the probability of a result at least as extreme as ours if the null hypothesis, “no effect”, is true) does not say that the tested hypothesis has a 3% chance of being true: this chapter teaches how to read what a test really says.

  1. Population, sample, variable, parameter and statistic; the type of scale (nominal, ordinal, interval, ratio) dictates which statistics are allowed.
  2. Graphical displays and measures of central tendency: mean, median, mode; the mean is sensitive to extreme values (one very rich person makes a “mean of millionaires”).
  3. Dispersion: sample variance s² = Σ(xi − x̄)²/(n − 1), where xi are the values, x̄ their mean and n their number; standard deviation s, range, quantiles.
  4. Notation used here: N(μ, σ²), where μ is the mean, σ the standard deviation and σ² the variance. Caution: the slides write N(m, s), with the standard deviation second. Skewness and kurtosis describe other aspects of the shape of the distribution.
  5. Jarque-Bera: JB = (n/6)[S² + (K − 3)²/4], where n is the number of values, S the skewness and K the raw kurtosis (equal to 3 for a normal distribution), not excess kurtosis. Under the asymptotic conditions of the test, the 5% threshold is about 5.99 (χ² distribution with 2 degrees of freedom, the parameter that sets its shape). Try the normality test from this chapter. Jarque–Bera test.
  6. Standardized variable z = (x − μ)/σ and the table of N(0, 1); about 95% of the values lie between μ − 1.96σ and μ + 1.96σ.
  7. 95% confidence interval of the mean: x̄ ± 1.96 · s/√n for large n.
  8. Central limit theorem: for independent observations with the same distribution and a finite variance, the mean, once centred and scaled, tends to a normal distribution; the standard error of the mean (its standard deviation from one sample to another) is σ/√n. Dependence requires additional conditions.
  9. Hypothesis testing: the null hypothesis H₀ (no effect) against the alternative H₁, one- or two-sided test, risk α of wrongly rejecting H₀, test statistic and p-value, the probability under H₀ of a result at least as extreme (a small one flags a result poorly compatible with H₀); in the worked example, only one of three reading scores is significantly below the reference.
  10. Two independent samples, with means x̄1 and x̄2, standard deviations s1 and s2 and sizes n1 and n2: t = (x̄1 − x̄2)/√(s1²/n1 + s2²/n2) is the Welch statistic, with approximate Welch-Satterthwaite degrees of freedom (df). The paired test works on the differences; Student's test with equal variances uses a pooled variance.

I. Basic concepts

Read
Population
the whole set of individuals the question is about (all adults with hypertension, for example).
Sample
the part of the population actually observed; inference goes from the sample to the population.
Variable
a characteristic measured on each individual (blood group, pain grade, weight).
Parameter and statistic
a parameter describes the population and is usually unknown (written with Greek letters: μ, σ); a statistic is computed on the sample (Latin letters: x̄, s) and estimates it.

Descriptive statistics summarize the data observed; inferential statistics draw conclusions about the population, with a measured uncertainty.

Measurement scales decide which calculations make sense:

  • nominal: categories without order (blood group); counts, proportions and the mode;
  • ordinal: ordered categories (a pain grade, a cancer stage); the median and quantiles become meaningful too;
  • interval: equal differences but an arbitrary zero (temperature in °C); means and standard deviations are allowed, ratios are not;
  • ratio: a true zero (weight, a concentration); all the usual statistics, including ratios.

II. Describing data

Read

Graphs come first: bar or pie charts for categories, histograms and box plots for measurements. They show the shape of the data, its center, its spread and the outliers before any number is computed. In the formulas, n is the number of values, xi the i-th value and Σ the sum over all of them.

Central tendency. The mean x̄ = Σxi/n; the median, the middle value of the sorted data; the mode, the most frequent value. The slides' trap: in a group where one person is extremely rich, the mean wealth exceeds one million euros, yet nobody else is a millionaire. The median, about €30,000, describes the group far better. The mean is pulled by extreme values; the median is not.

Dispersion. Range (maximum − minimum), quantiles (the median splits the data in two halves, the quartiles in four quarters), variance and standard deviation.

Worked example from the slides. The 11 values 5, 6, …, 15 have a mean of 10. Their deviations from the mean, −5 to +5, add up to 0, which is why deviations are squared. The squared deviations add up to 110; their mean, 110/11 = 10, is the variance computed with n (population formula). The sample variance divides by n − 1: s² = 110/10 = 11, and s = √11 ≈ 3.3.

The synthesis slide pairs each population parameter with the sample statistic that estimates it: μ with x̄, σ² with s², σ with s.

III. The normal distribution

Read

The normal (Gaussian) distribution is the symmetric bell curve set by two numbers, the mean μ and the standard deviation σ. Its density is f(x) = exp(−(x − μ)²/(2σ²)) / (σ√(2π)). Two further moments describe the shape of real data: the skewness S (third moment, asymmetry) and the kurtosis K (fourth moment, weight of the tails), equal to 0 and 3 for a normal distribution.

Is my sample normal? Look first (histogram, normal probability plot), then test. The slides compute the Jarque-Bera statistic in a spreadsheet: if JB < 5.99, normality is not rejected at the 5% level (n > 20). The data are then compatible with a normal distribution, which does not prove that they follow one.

Standardizing. z = (x − μ)/σ turns any normal variable into the standard normal N(0, 1), whose table gives P(Z < z). With the slides' N(50, 10), a value of 70 lies two standard deviations above the mean: z = 2. About 68% of values lie within one standard deviation of the mean, 95% within 1.96 and 99% within 2.58. Explore these areas in the normal distribution experiment.

Confidence interval for a mean. The standard error of the mean, s/√n, measures how much x̄ varies from one sample to another. For a large sample, the 95% confidence interval is x̄ ± 1.96 s/√n.

Worked example: cholesterol in 100 people, grouped in six classes centered on 120, 160, 200, 240, 280 and 320, with counts 9, 22, 25, 21, 16 and 7. Mean: 21 360/100 = 213.6. Sum of the weighted squared deviations: 311 104, hence a variance of 3111.04 and a standard deviation of about 55.8. Standard error: 55.8/√100 ≈ 5.58. 95% confidence interval: 213.6 ± 1.96 × 5.58 = 213.6 ± 10.9, that is about 202.7 to 224.5.

IV. Hypotheses

Read

Sampling distribution. Draw many samples of size n: their means vary around μ, with a standard deviation σ/√n that shrinks as n grows. This distribution of means is what a test reasons about.

The steps of a test.

  1. State the null hypothesis H₀ (no effect, no difference) and the alternative H₁.
  2. Choose a two-sided test (a difference in either direction) or a one-sided test (a single direction, decided before seeing the data).
  3. Set the risk α of rejecting H₀ when it is true (type I error), often 5%. Failing to reject a false H₀ is a type II error.
  4. Compute the test statistic and compare it with the critical value of its distribution, or compute the p-value.
  5. Conclude: reject H₀, or say that the data do not allow rejecting it, which is not proof that H₀ is true.

Worked example: three reading scores. Do the scores of three patients (X₁ = 40, X₂ = 35, X₃ = 30) fall below the population reference, μ₀ = 50 with σ = 10? H₀: μ = 50 against H₁: μ < 50 (one-sided, to the left). The z-scores are −1, −1.5 and −2; the 5% one-sided critical value is −1.645. One-sided p-values: about 0.16, 0.067 and 0.023. Only the third patient has a score significantly below the reference; the first two remain compatible with normal variability.

V. Hypothesis testing (means)

Read

The central limit theorem justifies working with means: whatever the distribution of the data (with a finite variance), the mean of a large sample is approximately normal.

z-test or t-test. When σ is known, or n is large, compare a mean with the normal distribution (z-test). When σ is estimated by s on a small sample, use Student's t-test: t = (x̄ − μ₀)/(s/√n), with n − 1 degrees of freedom (df). The decision compares |t| with the value read in the t table, or uses the p-value given by a spreadsheet function such as T.TEST.

Conditions. Independent observations, distributions close to normal or samples large enough, and, for the classic two-sample test, similar variances (checked with an F test); otherwise the unequal-variance (Welch) version is used. Two independent groups call for an unpaired test; two measurements on the same subjects (before and after) call for a paired test on the differences. Try the two-sample test in the Student's t-test experiment.

Worked example: left and right Broca's area. A measurement called GFI on the slides is taken in Broca's area, on the left and on the right, in 12 subjects for each side. Means 36 and 70.25; variances 88.73 and 22.02 (standard deviations 9.42 and 4.69). t = (36 − 70.25)/√(88.73/12 + 22.02/12) ≈ −11.27, with df = 22. The critical value at 5% (two-sided) is 2.074; since 11.27 > 2.074, H₀ is rejected, and the difference is highly significant.

The chapter ends with an overview of the parametric tests, those that assume a distribution for the data, to choose the right test for the question.

The words of the chapter

Read

Each term and abbreviation of the chapter, as used in the course.

Variance, standard deviation
The sample variance s² is the sum of squared deviations from the mean, divided by n − 1; the standard deviation s is its square root.
Standardized variable (z)
z = (x − μ)/σ: the deviation from the mean, counted in standard deviations.
95% confidence interval
For a mean and large n: x̄ ± 1.96 · s/√n.
Null hypothesis (H0)
The “no effect” hypothesis that the test tries to challenge.
p-value
The probability of observing a result at least as extreme as ours if the null hypothesis is true.
Jarque-Bera test
A normality test based on skewness S and kurtosis K (equal to 3 for a normal distribution); the tool on this site writes the same formula with the excess C = K − 3.

Going further: questions and answers

Why divide by n − 1 and not by n?

The deviations are measured from the sample mean, which is as close to them as possible: dividing by n underestimates the population variance in expectation. Dividing by n − 1 corrects this bias, because one degree of freedom was used to estimate the mean.

Does a p-value of 0.03 give the probability that H0 is true?

No. Under H0 and the assumptions of the model, it is the probability of a statistic at least as extreme as the one observed, for the chosen test. It is neither P(H0 | data) nor a measure of the practical importance of the effect. A small one only says that the data are poorly compatible with H0.

When is Student's distribution used for a mean?

For independent normal observations with an unknown variance, (x̄ − μ0)/(s/√n) follows exactly a Student distribution with n − 1 degrees of freedom (df), even for large n. Without normality, the approximation depends on the size and on the distribution; two-group and paired tests have their own conditions.

Is a normality test enough to choose a method?

No: a large sample detects small departures and a small sample lacks power. One must look at the graphs, the extreme values, the independence and the inference question; the central limit theorem does not repair biased data collection or any dependence.
