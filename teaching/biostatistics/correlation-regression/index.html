Chapter 3: correlation, linear regression and χ² tests

Covariance and Pearson correlation, the regression line and R², the significance of r, the χ² goodness-of-fit and independence tests, with exercises.

Slides (69, in French) · PDF in French (5.1 MB) · Questions

The chapter in 10 points

Key point. With 1000 pairs, r (the correlation coefficient, between −1 and 1) = 0.1 can be significant (the zero-correlation hypothesis is rejected at the chosen level) while explaining only 1% of the variation: significant does not mean useful.

  1. The scatter plot shows the shape of the link; covariance (how two variables vary together; Σ is the sum over the n measured pairs, xᵢ and yᵢ their values, x̄ and ȳ the means of the two series) cov(x, y) = Σ(xi − x̄)(yi − ȳ)/(n − 1).
  2. Pearson correlation: r = cov(x, y)/(sx sy), where sx and sy are the standard deviations, between −1 and 1, to be interpreted with care (non-linear link, outliers, causation).
  3. Least-squares line: b1 = cov(x, y)/sx², b0 = ȳ − b1 x̄, slope b1 and intercept b0; it is used to predict y from x.
  4. In simple least-squares linear regression with an intercept, R² = r² measures the share of the variation of y explained in the sample; this equality does not carry over to every model.
  5. Under H0: ρ = 0 (the null hypothesis: no correlation), where ρ is the correlation in the population, for independent pairs from a bivariate normal distribution, t = r√(n − 2)/√(1 − r²) follows a Student distribution with n − 2 df (degrees of freedom: the number of free values in the calculation). A non-linear link can escape Pearson's r.
  6. Residuals (the gaps between the observed values and the line), tests on the slope and on the intercept, and the 95% prediction interval around a predicted value.
  7. Qualitative variables: χ² = Σ (O − E)²/E, where O are the observed counts and E the expected counts.
  8. χ² goodness of fit: k − 1 degrees of freedom (df) for k probabilities fixed in advance; estimating m parameters can bring the df down to k − 1 − m under the conditions of the test. Example from the course: 52.75 exceeds 9.488 for 4 df at the 5% level.
  9. χ² test of independence on a contingency table, with (R − 1)(C − 1) degrees of freedom for R rows and C columns: treatment and recovery, nationality and brand, sex and site of a cancer.
  10. The synthesis exercises chain comparison of means, correlation, regression line and prediction interval on data from smokers.

To check the conditions of the models: NIST: least squares and residual variance · NIST: confidence and prediction intervals · NIST: conditions of the χ² goodness-of-fit test.

I. Correlation

Read

Always start with the scatter plot: one point per pair (xi, yi). It shows the direction of the link, its form (straight, curved, none), its strength and the outliers.

The covariance says whether x and y tend to move together (positive) or in opposite directions (negative), but its value depends on the units. Pearson's correlation coefficient r divides it by the two standard deviations: it has no unit and lies between −1 and 1. Spreadsheet functions compute it directly. The slides also show an adjusted r, which corrects the optimism of r in small samples.

The trap. r measures only a linear link: a strong curved relationship can give r close to 0, and a strong r says nothing about causation.

II. Linear regression

Read

Simple linear regression is the first learning model: two parameters are fitted on data in order to predict. The least-squares line ŷ = b0 + b1 x is the line that minimizes the sum of the squared vertical gaps between the points and the line, with slope b1 = cov(x, y)/sx² and intercept b0 = ȳ − b1 x̄. Once fitted, it predicts y for a new x, preferably inside the range of x already observed. Spreadsheets give the slope, the intercept and their statistics (SLOPE, INTERCEPT, LINEST).

The coefficient of determination R² is the share of the variation of y explained by the line; in simple regression with an intercept, R² = r². See how a single point moves the line in the influential point experiment.

III. Significance

Read

Is r different from 0? Under H₀: ρ = 0, compute t = r√(n − 2)/√(1 − r²) and compare it with the t table for n − 2 df.

Significant is not useful. With n = 1000 and r = 0.1: t = 0.1 × √998/√0.99 ≈ 3.2, above 1.96, so ρ = 0 is rejected at 5%; yet R² = 0.01, 1% of the variation explained. Compare the p-value and R² in the significance and R² experiment.

Outliers. One point far from the others can create a correlation that does not exist in the rest of the data, or hide one that does.

Residuals and tests on the line. The residual of a point is ei = yi − ŷi; the residual variance se² = Σei²/(n − 2) measures the scatter around the line. The slope and the intercept are tested with t statistics (estimate divided by its standard error, n − 2 df); a slope significantly different from 0 means that x helps to predict y. The slides apply this to blood pressure.

Prediction interval. 95% of new observations should fall within ŷ₀ ± t × se × √(1 + 1/n + (x₀ − x̄)²/Sxx), where Sxx = Σ(xi − x̄)². The band widens away from x̄. The slides use a simplified form on skin-cancer mortality (deaths per million inhabitants) in the United States. Compare the band for the mean and the band for a new observation in the regression intervals experiment.

IV. χ² goodness of fit

Read

For qualitative variables, the χ² (chi-square) test compares observed counts O with the counts E expected under H₀: χ² = Σ (O − E)²/E. A goodness-of-fit test checks whether the observed counts follow a given distribution. The rule: if the computed χ² exceeds the table value, H₀ is rejected.

Worked example: candy flavors. The five flavors are counted in 10 bags of 100 candies. H₀: all flavors are in equal proportions; H₁: they are not. The risk α is set at 5%, accepting a 5% risk of concluding that the flavors are unequal when they are equal. The computed χ² is 52.75; with 5 − 1 = 4 df, the table gives 9.488. Since 52.75 > 9.488, H₀ is rejected: the bags do not contain each flavor equally. Lemon lovers will find more of it; cherry lovers, less.

Try a die and a 2×2 table in the chi-square test experiment.

V. χ² test of independence

Read

The test of independence asks whether two qualitative variables are linked. Under H₀ (independence), the expected count of each cell is E = (row total × column total) / grand total. The χ² is computed over all cells and compared with the table for (R − 1)(C − 1) degrees of freedom, R rows and C columns. The slides work a simple example, then a second one, each with its rejection region. The expected counts must not be too small for the approximation to hold.

VI. Exercises

Read

The exercises review the three chapters. When the slides give the result, it is reported here.

  • Probability. 120 of 1000 people are sick: probability that a person drawn at random is sick, then that 5 people drawn with replacement are all healthy (independent draws, so the probabilities multiply). A couple wants 4 children, 3 girls and 1 boy, with P(boy) = 0.51: probability if the boy must be the last child, and if he can be at any rank (4 possible ranks). The third exercise computes a PPV and an NPV.
  • Correlation and regression. Is the weight of pandas linked to the altitude where they live? Then: the equation of a regression line, the sum of squared residuals and a test of the slope.
  • Comparing means: cholesterol in men and women. 34 men (mean 2.30, SD 0.55) and 32 women (mean 2.18, SD 0.54), two independent groups, hence an unpaired Student's t-test. Two-sided p = 0.357 (equal or unequal variances), and the F test of the variances gives p = 0.879: no difference is shown at the 5% level. The spreadsheet function is T.TEST(range1, range2, tails, type), type 1 = paired, 2 = equal variances, 3 = unequal variances. The paired test, correlation and regression also shown on the slide only illustrate the syntax: they make no statistical sense for two independent groups.
  • Goodness of fit: random digits. 250 random numbers; each final digit 0–9 is expected 25 times. Observed: 17, 31, 29, 18, 14, 20, 35, 30, 20, 36. χ² = 582/25 ≈ 23.3 > 16.92 (9 df, 5%): the table of random numbers is suspect.
  • Independence: a serum for a skin disease. With the serum, 75 of 100 patients recover; without it, 65 of 100. Expected counts under H₀: 70 recovered and 30 not in each group. χ² = 2 × 25/70 + 2 × 25/30 ≈ 2.38, below 3.84 (1 df, 5%): H₀ cannot be rejected, and the serum is not shown to be effective.
  • Independence: nationality and hockey-stick brand. With 20 df, the critical value is 31.41; the computed χ² is lower, so no link between nationality and brand is shown.
  • Independence: sex and site of a stomach cancer (pylorus, body, cardia). Men 53, 66, 75; women 48, 33, 23 (298 patients). With the expected counts, χ² ≈ 12.8 > 5.99 (2 df, 5%): H₀ is rejected, and the site of the cancer is linked to the sex of the patient.
  • Synthesis: thiocyanate (SCN⁻) in smokers. The mean concentrations of smokers and non-smokers differ (p < 10⁻⁹); then the 95% confidence interval of the mean in smokers; the correlation between the serum (Z) and saliva (Y) concentrations, with t far above the table value 2.042 (33 df, read at 30), so they are correlated; finally the least-squares line Y = b0 + b1Z, a prediction for a serum concentration of 200 and its 95% prediction interval when the residual variance is 0.75.

Going further: questions and answers

What is the difference between a confidence interval and a prediction interval?

In simple linear regression with independent normal errors of constant variance: 95% confidence interval (CI) of the mean at x0 = ŷ0 ± t(n−2; 0.975) se √[1/n + (x0−x̄)²/Sxx], where ŷ0 is the value predicted by the line at x0 and t(n−2; 0.975) the Student quantile with n − 2 degrees of freedom. For a future observation, add 1 under the root. Here Sxx = Σ(xi−x̄)² and se² = Σresidual²/(n−2). The prediction interval therefore keeps the individual scatter; 1.96 is only an asymptotic approximation of the quantile.

Is a significant r enough to make a prediction useful?

No: with 1000 pairs, r = 0.1 can be significant while explaining only 1% of the variation in a simple regression with an intercept. Rejecting ρ = 0 under a model proves neither causation nor predictive usefulness; the latter is also measured on data not used to fit the model.

Why can a single outlier change everything?

Least squares weigh the square of the deviations: a point far away in x can strongly change the fitted slope and correlation, increasing or decreasing the apparent relationship depending on its position. Always look at the scatter plot and the residuals before concluding.

What to do when the expected counts of a χ² are small?

The asymptotic approximation can be poor; examine the size and structure of the table. A justified grouping or an exact method may be suitable, notably Fisher's test for a 2×2 table in its setting. Yates' continuity correction does not guarantee to solve the small-count problem.
