Which baseline must a solar model beat? When is deep learning worth it?

Start with persistence (it repeats the last observed value), then a phase-aware reference, an autoregression (the next value predicted from a linear combination of recent ones) and a frugal learner (a small model fitted in one calculation). Keep a deep network only if it brings a measured gain on the same protocol. Deep learning can be worth it. It has to show this against references that cost almost nothing to run.

Cyril Voyant · Mines Paris – PSL, Centre O.I.E. · 2 November 2026

In brief

  • Test in order: persistence, a reference that knows the cycle, an autoregression, a frugal learner, and only then a deep network.
  • In Ajaccio, one hour ahead, each step gains little: daytime relative error of 22.0 % (smart persistence), 21.3 % (autoregression) then 20.5 % (ELM), while cost and data needs go up.
  • Keep a deep network if it does better on the same protocol, with its cost reported.

Reading the quantities.

  • MAE is mean absolute error; RMSE is root mean squared error and gives large errors more weight. nRMSE normalizes RMSE by the mean observation on the evaluated data.
  • AR is autoregression; ELM (extreme learning machine) fits output weights while keeping a fixed random hidden layer.
  • sCV (stochastic coefficient of variation) measures how far irradiance departs from the clear-sky curve: 0 under a clear sky, 1 for a sky that alternates at random between clear sky and thick cloud.
  • F (forecastability) lies between 1 − sCV, when those departures keep no memory, and 1, when they are perfectly correlated in time; unlike sCV, it depends on the horizon (the delay between the last known value and the time forecast).
  • Both are explained, with an interactive page, in Which forecast for which signal?; the study is summarized in sCV and F, explained; equations and code are on the methods page.

What to test, in order

  1. Simple persistence. The last observed value: the minimal reference of any forecast.
  2. A reference that knows the cycle. Clear-sky-index persistence (the last clear-sky index, irradiance divided by clear-sky irradiance, repeated), or BLEND, a convex combination (two positive weights adding up to 1) of immediate memory and the value at the same phase of the cycle. BLEND works from raw irradiance and its weights are in closed form (Voyant et al., 2026).
  3. An autoregression. Fitted by least squares (minimizing the sum of squared errors) on recent lags.
  4. A frugal learner. An Extreme Learning Machine (Huang, Zhu and Siew, 2006) keeps a random hidden layer and fits its output weights analytically.
  5. A deep network. LSTM (long short-term memory, a network that remembers sequences), U-Net (a network designed to read images) or larger, under the same split, horizons and tuning budget (the number of settings tried).

Two results: a teaching example and a published study

Ajaccio, 2007 test year, one hour ahead (UE14, the solar forecasting course). The daytime nRMSE is 22.0 % for smart persistence (here the last gap to the clear-sky curve is carried forward, as in the course), 21.3 % for AR(2) (autoregression on the last two values) and 20.5 % for an ELM. The steps are small, and each is measured.

Corsican electricity system, six years of hourly data (Applied Energy, 2026). A multiple-output (MIMO) ELM is compared with persistence, single-output formulations and an LSTM under a realistic tuning budget. One hour ahead, nRMSE is 0.179 for solar generation.

What counts as a gain

  • The same targets, scope and horizon for all models.
  • Training rows kept apart from evaluation rows, including any scaling (normalisation of the inputs).
  • A difference tested across stations or periods, not only an average.
  • Costs reported with the error: parameters, training time, what the device can store.
  • The signal described next to the score: with sCV and F (see the box above), a reader can tell a regular sky from a hard-to-forecast one.

Limits

These results concern the datasets and protocols studied. BLEND does not outperform every optimized method; autoregressive or trained predictors can do better under some conditions. Nothing here is a universal ranking.
