Atmospheric neutrons and boron capture: how far can the idea go?

At aircraft altitude, neutrons can indeed trigger captures in a boron-10-enriched tumour, but our 2011 calculation gives about 1 µGy (one millionth of a gray, the unit of energy absorbed per kilogram) per hour on average: reaching 1 Gy would take a million hours. The mechanism exists; the mean dose is not enough to treat a visible tumour. You will see how a model quantifies an idea before promising an application.

Explore the model · Read the finding · Draw neutrons · Paper and team

The finding: a quantified limit, not a flying hospital

The paper’s case: 12 km altitude, 45° latitude, 14.7 neutrons per cm² per second, 10 cm of water slowing the neutrons, 0.5 cm of skull and a tumour of about 1 g with 20 µg of boron-10 per gram. A hybrid model, Monte Carlo transport (random draws of neutron paths) with analytical approximations, estimates a mean tumour dose of the order of 1 µGy for one hour. That is insufficient to treat a visible tumour.

The sliders do not redo this calculation: they scale the published result, at fixed spectrum, geometry and mass. Duration multiplies the dose and the interactions. Boron multiplies the interactions only, because the paper quantifies the dose at 20 µg/g only. This tumour is a phantom, that is a numerical model of tissue: its concentration is not an amount to administer to a person.

Three ideas to follow everything

BNCT means “boron neutron capture therapy”. A compound carries boron-10 to the target tissue; when this boron captures a neutron, an alpha particle and a lithium-7 nucleus deposit their energy over a few micrometres. Neutrons do not recognize a tumour: localization depends on the boron distribution as much as on the neutron field. The IAEA introduces BNCT principles and compounds.

BPA (4-borono-L-phenylalanine, a boron-bearing amino-acid analogue) and BSH (sodium borocaptate, Na₂B₁₂H₁₁SH, a cluster of 12 boron atoms) are the two boron carriers of the paper. They are not neutron sources. Their distribution in the body is a biological question, separate from the nuclear reaction.

A cross section σ(E) says how readily a nucleus reacts with a neutron of energy E. It is expressed as an area, often in barns (1 barn = 10⁻²⁴ cm²), without being the size of the nucleus or, by itself, the probability that a flight neutron is captured: that also depends on the number of nuclei, the flux and the neutron energy.

From the cosmos to the cell

Galactic cosmic rays, mostly energetic protons and nuclei, produce secondary particles in the atmosphere, including neutrons. Altitude and the geomagnetic field matter. The galactic background is generally higher at solar minimum. The tool’s diagram is qualitative, not a space-weather calculation.

What the study contributes to modeling

The work connects three levels: reactions in boron carrier molecules, transport through a moderator and tissue, and dose orders of magnitude. Its second contribution replaces some expensive transport steps with analytical expressions for transmission and mean free path. The paper reports 42 seconds instead of 15 hours for 6,000 neutrons, on the computers of the time: a concrete example of physical simplification serving a scientific question.

Monte Carlo: following many neutron histories

Multiplied by the number of nuclei per unit volume, the cross section gives the macroscopic cross section Σ, an inverse length; for a mixture, Σ adds these products nucleus by nucleus. Its inverse L = 1/Σ is the mean free path: the average distance between two collisions. A number U drawn at random between 0 and 1 then gives the distance travelled by a neutron, −L·ln(U). The code then chooses the nucleus and the reaction, follows energy and direction, starts again, and adds up the energy deposits. Try it: the paper uses 6,000 to 60,000 neutrons.

More histories reduce the statistical error, as 1/√N for N histories. They correct neither a wrong geometry nor wrong physical data.

The paper’s data and code

The code, written in Visual Basic, uses cross sections from the ENDF/B (Evaluated Nuclear Data File) library, accessed at the time through t2.lanl.gov at Los Alamos; it is produced by the CSEWG group (Cross Section Evaluation Working Group) and distributed by Brookhaven’s NNDC (National Nuclear Data Center). The paper mentions MCNP (Monte Carlo N-Particle) as an example of a transport code; our simulation does not use it.

Why does (n,p) appear as approximately zero?

In this notation, (n,p) means “absorbed, a proton leaves” and (n,α) “absorbed, an alpha particle leaves”. The zero shown is a rounded result for the neutron hitting the BSH or BPA molecule, not a physical impossibility. In tissue, ¹⁴N(n,p)¹⁴C also contributes to BNCT dose (IAEA, Table 7); the tool does not calculate this component. On the diagram, the skull thickness is exaggerated for visibility.

BSH versus BPA: what the comparison actually means

Table 2 of the paper compares the reactions during the interaction between a neutron and the molecule: the (n,α) channel accounts for 94% with BSH and 88% with BPA. These shares assume the interaction takes place: they give neither the capture probability of a flight neutron, nor tumour selectivity, nor a clinical success rate.

Very local does not mean everywhere

Capture gives ¹⁰B + n → ⁷Li + α: the particles travel about 4 µm for lithium and 8 µm for alpha. The paper also reports about 0.5 Gy per impact locally at cellular scale. This value holds for the cell that is hit, not for all cells. The tool does not recalculate this scale.

Flight exposure is a different quantity

For a simplified 56.5 kg cylinder, the paper obtains 4.37 µSv/h (microsieverts per hour), against 6.1 µSv/h for the SIEVERT tool (the French service that estimates the dose received during a flight) on a Paris–New York flight: about 28% lower, with different solar periods and approximations. It is an order-of-magnitude comparison, not a current flight calculator. Gray (Gy) measures absorbed energy per unit mass, sievert (Sv) weights exposure: these two figures do not convert directly. To go from a dose to an effect in radiotherapy, see the LQ–LQL equivalent dose calculation.

Low mean dose, high LET: why the biological question remains interesting

Mean dose does not describe the entire microdosimetry. Alpha particles and recoiling lithium have high linear energy transfer (LET: energy deposited per unit track length), with dense ionisations and potentially complex DNA lesions. A capture can therefore have an important effect in its immediate surroundings. High LET does not create additional captures or guarantee that every tumour cell is hit: deposition quality and event rarity must both be considered.

Bystander effects: responses in neighbouring cells

An irradiated cell can transmit signals that change neighbouring cells not directly traversed by the charged particles. This biological response neither extends the alpha track nor creates absorbed dose at a distance. BNCT experiments have detected mutations in neighbouring cells (Kinashi et al., 2009). Another study found that conditioned medium from treated cells promoted migration of non-irradiated cells without changing their proliferation under the reported conditions (Rodriguez et al., 2025). Bystander signalling is therefore not automatically antitumour killing.

The research scenario: residual disease and recurrence

The hypothesis is an adjuvant contribution against microscopic residual disease, to be evaluated for a possible effect on recurrence risk. It extends the micrometastasis question in our 2011 paper: combine very local high-LET deposition with possible neighbour responses. This is neither demonstrated recurrence prevention nor a validated treatment of an established tumour. The cited biological experiments use irradiation conditions different from our atmospheric exposure; they do not establish benefit at approximately 1 µGy/h in our phantom.

The experimental question is whether such rare captures actually reduce residual cells’ ability to regrow a tumour, with a favourable normal-tissue balance. Damage markers or mutations alone cannot answer this. Boron distribution, clonogenic survival (a cell’s ability to regrow a colony) and, in suitable preclinical models, longitudinal tumour control and toxicity would need investigation. Systemic immune or abscopal responses (away from the irradiated area) should be distinguished from local bystander effects.

Hypoxia and a poorly vascularised core: separate the obstacles

Hypoxia means insufficient oxygen; ischaemia means insufficient perfusion. High-LET effects are less oxygen-dependent than low-LET photon effects, but this advantage does not solve boron delivery. A poorly perfused core may receive less carrier, with heterogeneous distribution. Experimental hypoxia also reduced BPA uptake and LAT1 amino-acid transporter expression in glioblastoma cells (Wada et al., 2018).

High LET alone therefore does not resolve a hypoxic or poorly vascularised core. Conversely, microscopic foci can themselves be hypoxic or avascular: small size does not guarantee boron accessibility. The adjuvant scenario remains an interesting research question, conditional on investigating captures, microdistribution and biological responses at the actual dose rate proposed.

Radiobiological context: IAEA, Current Status of Neutron Capture Therapy, §6.6. The cell-signalling diagram is qualitative: it calculates no cell-kill count, bystander range or percentage of recurrences prevented. This page describes a research hypothesis, not a treatment protocol.
