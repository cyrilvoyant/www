Atmospheric neutrons and boron capture: how far can the idea go?

In 2011, we asked an unusual question: could neutrons at aircraft altitude trigger captures in a boron-10-enriched tumour? The mechanism exists, but our calculation found a mean dose too low to treat a macroscopic tumour. The contribution is to quantify that limit before promising an application.

Before the experiment: three key ideas

BNCT means “boron neutron capture therapy”. It combines a compound that carries boron-10 to the target tissue with neutrons. After boron-10 captures a neutron, the reaction produces an alpha particle and a lithium-7 nucleus; they deposit their energy over a few micrometres. Localization therefore depends on the boron distribution as well as on the neutron field: neutrons do not identify tumour cells by themselves. The IAEA introduces BNCT principles and compounds.

In our paper, BPA means 4-borono-L-phenylalanine, a boron-bearing amino-acid analogue; BSH is sodium borocaptate, a cluster compound containing 12 boron atoms (Na₂B₁₂H₁₁SH). These are two boron carriers studied in the paper, not neutron sources. How each compound distributes in the body is a biological question separate from the nuclear reaction.

A cross section σ(E) quantifies how readily a nucleus reacts with a neutron of energy E. It is expressed in units of area, often barns, but is neither the nucleus’s geometric size nor, by itself, the probability that a flight neutron is captured in a tumour. That probability also depends on the number of target nuclei, neutron flux and energy spectrum. The Monte Carlo section explains how σ leads to an interaction probability in matter.

Where this fits in my research

Medical physics and solar forecasting share a method: start with the phenomenon, state geometry and assumptions, simulate, and check whether the order of magnitude meets the need. This question does not require AI. A model also helps recognize an insufficient approach and identify what remains to be demonstrated.

An atmospheric cascade, then a cellular-scale reaction

Galactic cosmic rays, mainly energetic protons and nuclei, generate secondary particles, including neutrons, in the atmosphere. Altitude, the geomagnetic field and particle spectra matter. Solar wind modulates incoming cosmic rays; it is distinct from solar energetic particle events. The galactic background is generally higher at solar minimum. The finding: a quantitative limit, not a validated flying hospital

The paper’s reference case uses 12 km altitude, 45° latitude, a neutron flux of 14.7 cm⁻²·s⁻¹, 10 cm of water, a 0.5 cm skull layer and an approximately 1 g tumour with 20 µg of boron-10 per gram. A hybrid model combines Monte Carlo transport with analytical approximations. The estimated mean tumour dose is of the order of 1 µGy for one hour, insufficient for macroscopic therapeutic irradiation.

At the reference concentration only, the tool illustrates D̄ ≈ 1 µGy × (t / 1 h). A separate panel shows I = 100% × (t / 1 h) × (c₁₀B / 20 µg·g⁻¹): a relative boron-interaction count index, normalized to 100% for one hour at 20 µg/g. It is neither a capture probability nor a total dose at a different concentration. Spectrum, geometry and mass stay fixed; the paper’s transport model is not rerun. Concentration refers to a numerical phantom, not an amount to administer to a person.

What the study contributes to modeling

The work connects three levels: reactions in boron carrier molecules, transport through a moderator and tissue, and dose orders of magnitude. Its second contribution replaces some expensive transport steps with analytical expressions for transmission and mean free path. The paper reports 42 seconds instead of 15 hours for 6,000 neutrons, under its historical computing conditions. This is not a benchmark of this browser: it is a concrete example of physical simplification serving a scientific question.

Monte Carlo: following many neutron histories

A microscopic cross section σ(E) has units of area and characterizes interactions of a neutron of energy E with a nucleus. The material’s macroscopic cross section Σ = Σᵢ Nᵢσᵢ has units of inverse length, where Nᵢ is species i number density; it sets the mean free path 1/Σ. In a homogeneous medium, a uniform draw U between 0 and 1 gives a sampled distance ℓ = −ln(U)/Σ. The code then samples the nucleus and reaction channel from cross sections and follows the energy and direction after collision. Many histories are accumulated to estimate energy deposition.

Under the usual independent-history, finite-variance assumptions, standard error decreases as 1/√N. More histories do not correct inappropriate geometry or physical data. Our 2011 study combines these draws with analytical expressions; the browser diagram illustrates the mechanism without rerunning the transport.

Los Alamos, ENDF/B and the paper’s code

The paper used ENDF/B data, citing access through Los Alamos’ historical t2.lanl.gov website. These evaluations supply cross sections and reaction distributions. ENDF/B is produced by CSEWG and distributed by Brookhaven’s NNDC, rather than belonging exclusively to Los Alamos. Our code was written in Visual Basic. The paper mentions MCNP as an example of a transport code using these evaluations; this is not a claim that our simulation ran MCNP or involved an institutional partnership with Los Alamos.

BSH versus BPA: what the comparison actually means

BNCT means Boron Neutron Capture Therapy. BSH and BPA are boron carriers. Table 2 compares neutron–molecule reaction distributions in the studied spectrum: the (n,α) channel accounts for 94% with BSH and 88% with BPA. These conditional distributions are neither capture probabilities for each flight neutron, tumour selectivity nor clinical success rates. At equal boron-10 concentration, the tool therefore applies no carrier-dependent dose multiplier.

Very local does not mean everywhere

Capture produces ⁷Li and an alpha particle: ¹⁰B + n → ⁷Li + α. Their tracks are a few micrometres long (approximately 4 µm for lithium and 8 µm for alpha in the paper). The paper also reports about 0.5 Gy per impact locally at cellular scale. That conditional value does not mean that every cell receives this dose. This tool does not reproduce the microdosimetry: scoring volume and deposited-energy fraction would need clarification before recalculating it.

Flight exposure is a different quantity

For a simplified 56.5 kg cylinder, the paper obtains 4.37 µSv/h versus a historical SIEVERT Paris–New York reference of 6.1 µSv/h, approximately 28% lower, with different solar periods and several approximations. This order-of-magnitude comparison is not a current flight calculator. Gray (Gy) measures absorbed energy per unit mass; sievert (Sv) weights radiological exposure. The two panels cannot be directly converted into each other.

Low mean dose, high LET: why the biological question remains interesting

Mean dose does not describe the entire microdosimetry. Alpha particles and recoiling lithium have high linear energy transfer (LET: energy deposited per unit track length), with dense ionisations and potentially complex DNA lesions. A capture can therefore have an important effect in its immediate surroundings. High LET does not create additional captures or guarantee that every tumour cell is hit: deposition quality and event rarity must both be considered.

Bystander effects: responses in neighbouring cells

An irradiated cell can transmit signals that change neighbouring cells not directly traversed by the charged particles. This biological response neither extends the alpha track nor creates absorbed dose at a distance. BNCT experiments have detected mutations in neighbouring cells (Kinashi et al., 2009). Another study found that conditioned medium from treated cells promoted migration of non-irradiated cells without changing their proliferation under the reported conditions (Rodriguez et al., 2025). Bystander signalling is therefore not automatically antitumour killing.

The research scenario: residual disease and recurrence

The hypothesis is an adjuvant contribution against microscopic residual disease, to be evaluated for a possible effect on recurrence risk. It extends the micrometastasis question in our 2011 paper: combine very local high-LET deposition with possible neighbour responses. This is neither demonstrated recurrence prevention nor a validated treatment of an established tumour. The cited biological experiments use irradiation conditions different from our atmospheric exposure; they do not establish benefit at approximately 1 µGy/h in our phantom.

The experimental question is whether such rare captures actually reduce residual cells’ ability to regrow a tumour, with a favourable normal-tissue balance. Damage markers or mutations alone cannot answer this. Boron distribution, clonogenic survival and, in suitable preclinical models, longitudinal tumour control and toxicity would need investigation. Systemic immune or abscopal responses should be distinguished from local bystander effects.

Hypoxia and a poorly vascularised core: separate the obstacles

Hypoxia means insufficient oxygen; ischaemia means insufficient perfusion. High-LET effects are less oxygen-dependent than low-LET photon effects, but this advantage does not solve boron delivery. A poorly perfused core may receive less carrier, with heterogeneous distribution. Experimental hypoxia also reduced BPA uptake and LAT1 transporter expression in glioblastoma cells (Wada et al., 2018).

High LET alone therefore does not resolve a hypoxic or poorly vascularised core. Conversely, microscopic foci can themselves be hypoxic or avascular: small size does not guarantee boron accessibility. The adjuvant scenario remains an interesting research question, conditional on investigating captures, microdistribution and biological responses at the actual dose rate proposed.
