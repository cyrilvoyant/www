Which forecast for which signal?

There is no best forecast as such: it all depends on the signal. On a random signal, the mean wins. On Brownian motion, persistence wins. With a cycle, the horizon matters too: very close ahead persistence is hard to beat; further ahead, cyclic persistence exploits the cycle. Choose a signal, play with the sliders, and watch what changes.

What this introduces

  • The convex combination. No simple reference wins everywhere, hence the idea of weighing them, with positive weights adding up to 1. Persistence and the mean: CLIPER. Persistence and cyclic persistence: BLEND. See CLIPER and BLEND.
  • Deseasonalization. A known cycle is not forecast: remove it, forecast the rest, put it back.
  • Ratio to trend or difference to trend. Additive noise calls for the difference to trend, multiplicative noise for the ratio to trend. In solar forecasting, the ratio to the clear-sky trend is the clear-sky index.
  • Variability and forecastability. sCV and F quantify them, before any model.
  • Evaluation. An error only makes sense against a reference: this is what NICE measures. How to evaluate a forecast.

Definitions

Persistence
The forecast copies the last known value.
Cyclic persistence
The forecast copies the value one cycle earlier, at the same moment of the cycle.
Mean
The forecast is always the mean of the past.
Convex combination
Two forecasts weighed by positive weights adding up to 1, computed on the past. Persistence and the mean: CLIPER. Persistence and cyclic persistence: BLEND.
AR(2)
A regression on the last two known values. Raw: without removing the cycle. It measures what removing the cycle brings.
Difference to trend
We study y − c, the gap to the cycle c. Suited to additive noise.
Ratio to trend
We study y / c. Suited to multiplicative noise. With c as clear sky: the clear-sky index.
Brownian motion
Each value is the previous one plus a random draw: memory φ = 1.
Memory φ
The correlation between two neighbouring values of the noise. 0: none. 1: total.
Horizon
The number of steps between the last known value and the value to forecast.
nRMSE, nMAE
The root mean squared error and the mean absolute error, divided by the mean of the signal.
NICE
The error divided by that of persistence. Between 0 and 1: the forecast adds something.
sCV, F
The quantification of the variability and forecastability of a signal, before any model.

Three complementary studies, three questions

2015, Can we diagnose difficulty before choosing the forecaster?

In 2015, I studied this question with Ted Soubdhan, Philippe Lauret, Mathieu David and Marc Muselli. Our paper examines statistical parameters for anticipating solar forecast error. Mean absolute log return (MALR), borrowed from econometrics, describes relative changes. This empirical diagnostic does not replace testing the model at the intended site and horizon. Paper in Energy (2015).

2025, Estimate expected error from variability

With Philippe Lauret, Mathieu David, Josselin Le Gal La Salle, Elke Lorenz and Richard Perez, I studied how hourly variability σ(Δkc), the standard deviation of hourly changes in the clear-sky index kc, relates to RMSE (root mean squared error), using regressions calibrated on 60 sites for intra-day and day-ahead horizons. The 95% intervals are statistical, not universal physical limits. Paper in Solar Energy (2025).

sCV and F, Clear-sky departure versus temporal memory

sCV (stochastic coefficient of variation) normalizes the quadratic departure from clear sky. It does not by itself describe temporal ordering. F incorporates residual dependencies at lags compatible with the horizon. The same values and sCV can therefore coexist with different temporal structures and reference-model performance. Paper in Renewable Energy (2026) · sCV/F calculator · Reference code.

No free lunch: problem structure matters

Wolpert and Macready’s optimization no-free-lunch results establish equal average performance for the search algorithms considered when uniformly averaging over all functions on a finite problem. That precise setting is not the distribution of real solar forecasting tasks. It does not establish that all models are equivalent within a given domain or that simple models always win. Original reference (1997).

The connection is methodological: model assumptions must meet problem structure. Shuffling an example is not a proof of the theorem. It illustrates how the reference choice and horizon change the interpretation of error. At a horizon of 12 steps, anticorrelation may carry useful information while persistence, which copies without reversing, becomes inappropriate.

The message for a real decision

Understand the signal, establish a reference, measure improvement, then add complexity when the gain justifies it. Low error under an easy sky has a different meaning from comparable error under unstable conditions. High variability alone does not establish an absence of predictable structure. Examine the horizon, information available at forecast origin, out-of-training validation and model cost.

This connects to the atmospheric neutron study: simulation quantifies what a mechanism can offer, within its limits. Continue with NICE and persistence as a reference, BLEND, solar cycles and timestamps and the research thread.
