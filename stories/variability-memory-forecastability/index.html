Why the same model succeeds here and fails there

The same linear regression has an error of 11.8 % on a signal that keeps its memory, and 50.5 % on a signal that has none. The model did not change: the signal did. Before judging a model or making it more complex, find out what the signal allows you to forecast.

Error: nRMSE, the root mean squared error divided by the mean of the observations. Simulation below, presets “Almost predictable” and “No memory”, forecast one step ahead. Simulated values, not solar measurements.

What this means

  • When the signal has memory, copying the last value already does as well as the regression.
  • When it has none, copying the last value is the worst choice, and the regression does no better than a mean.
  • An error alone therefore does not judge a model. Compare it with a simple reference, on the same signal.

Solar forecasting raises the same question: a steady sky and a sky of changing clouds do not leave the same thing to forecast. Across 68 Spanish stations, our forecastability indicator F moves inversely to forecast error (sCV and F).

Main experiment: model, assumptions and equations

Here t denotes the current step and h the forecast lead. r controls memory between successive states; a is half the separation of the two levels; μ is their mean; β is the relative standard deviation of multiplicative noise and σa is the standard deviation of additive noise. St is −1 or +1 with switching probability q = (1−r)/2, initialized in its equiprobable stationary distribution. The signal before adding noise is Kt = μ + aSt, μ = 0.6, with exact autocorrelation rh when a > 0. Observations are Yt=Kt(1+βZm,t)+σaZa,t. The Z draws are independent of each other, across times and of the state, uniform on [−√3,+√3], with zero mean and unit variance. Multiplicative noise has conditional variance β²K², additive noise σa². Control ranges guarantee positive observations without clipping. This teaching generator is not a validated weather model.

The known-state reference knows the state at forecast origin: ŷref,t+h = μ + arhSt. Its exact expected squared risk is Rh=a2(1−r2h)+σa2+β2(μ2+a2), separating future transitions, additive noise and multiplicative noise. √Rh is an expected RMSE, not a threshold every finite sample must respect. The known-state reference has privileged information; it is not an operational forecast. This optimistic bound is specific to the generator, neither a numerical result of the cited papers nor a physical solar limit.

A is the line’s intercept and B its slope; B* denotes the theoretical optimal slope when the generator distribution is known. The h-step regression fits ŷ = A + BYt on training pairs only. Its stationary population-optimal linear slope is B*=a2rha2+σa2+β2(μ2+a2). Observation noise thus reduces memory accessible to a simple regression. Displayed A and B are fitted, not substituted from this formula. Training uses indices 0–599; common test targets 624–1199 follow a 24-step buffer. Every forecast origin is exactly h steps before its target and after training. Models see only Y. sCV/F are retrospective diagnostics using the whole series, Iclr = 1, d = 0.2 and conventional T = 24 for F.

MAE is mean absolute error; RMSE weights large errors more strongly. nRMSE = 100 × RMSE / mean test observation. NICE¹ and NICE² divide model MAE and RMSE by same-horizon simple-persistence errors on identical targets. NICEΣ equally averages the L¹, L² and L³ ratios, following the reference NICE code. Ratios are undefined for a perfect reference. NICE method and paper.

Three complementary studies, three questions

2015, Can we diagnose difficulty before choosing the forecaster?

In 2015, I studied this question with Ted Soubdhan, Philippe Lauret, Mathieu David and Marc Muselli. Our paper examines statistical parameters for anticipating solar forecast error. Mean absolute log return (MALR), borrowed from econometrics, describes relative changes. This empirical diagnostic does not replace testing the model at the intended site and horizon. Paper in Energy (2015).

2025, Estimate expected error from variability

With Philippe Lauret, Mathieu David, Josselin Le Gal La Salle, Elke Lorenz and Richard Perez, I studied how hourly variability σ(Δkc) relates to RMSE, using regressions calibrated on 60 sites for intra-day and day-ahead horizons. Philippe Lauret is first author; I am a co-author. The 95% intervals are statistical, not universal physical limits. Paper in Solar Energy (2025).

sCV and F, Clear-sky departure versus temporal memory

sCV normalizes the quadratic departure from clear sky. It does not by itself describe temporal ordering. F incorporates residual dependencies at lags compatible with the horizon. The same values and sCV can therefore coexist with different temporal structures and reference-model performance. Renewable Energy paper (2026) · sCV/F calculator · Reference code.

Appendix: identical values, different temporal order, including sCV/F equations

An experiment separating amplitude from temporal order

We construct a positive series with an artificial period of 24 and shuffle a copy of its values. Its histogram is unchanged. Two simple rules are compared: copy the value available at forecast origin, or use the mean estimated on the first half. The aim is to understand these rules and assumptions. These are synthetic values, not solar measurements or a new benchmark of the papers.

The equations behind the panels

The reference is constant here: Iclr = 1 and k = I/Iclr. This isolates temporal ordering; a real application needs the Sun’s cycle too. With Δkt = kt − kt−1, MALR = mean(|ln(kt/kt−1)|). All values here are positive; real-data treatment of zeros and nights must be explicit. Both σ(Δk) and MALR depend on temporal neighbours and change when values are shuffled.

In this experiment, sCV = √2/(1−d) × √mean[(k−1)²], with d = 0.2. The general formula normalizes by root-mean-square clear-sky irradiance. For residuals ε = I − Iclr, F(h) = 1 + sCV[ρmax(h) − 1], where ρmax is the maximum |ρ(ℓ)| for h ≤ ℓ ≤ T/2, here T = 24. Correlations use available pairs, as in the reference MATLAB code. The sCV normalization at 1 is a reference, not a ceiling to impose on every series.

For each test target t: ŷpers(t) = k(t−h) and ŷmean(t) = mean(k0:95). Both RMSEs use the same 72 targets, indices 120 to 191. After training on indices 0 to 95, a 24-step buffer ensures that the mean is fitted before every forecast origin, including horizon 12. The mean stays fixed; future targets do not re-estimate it. Conversely, sCV, F, MALR and σ(Δk) describe the entire constructed series retrospectively. Those are distinct uses of the data.

No free lunch: problem structure matters

Wolpert and Macready’s optimization no-free-lunch results establish equal average performance for the search algorithms considered when uniformly averaging over all functions on a finite problem. That precise setting is not the distribution of real solar forecasting tasks. It does not establish that all models are equivalent within a given domain or that simple models always win. Original reference (1997).

The connection is methodological: model assumptions must meet problem structure. Shuffling an example is not a proof of the theorem. It illustrates how the reference choice and horizon change the interpretation of error. At horizon 12, anticorrelation may carry useful information while persistence, which copies without reversing, becomes inappropriate.

The message for a real decision

Understand the signal, establish a reference, measure improvement, then add complexity when the gain justifies it. Low error under an easy sky has a different meaning from comparable error under unstable conditions. High variability alone does not establish an absence of predictable structure. Examine the horizon, information available at forecast origin, out-of-training validation and model cost.

This connects to the atmospheric neutron study: simulation quantifies what a mechanism can offer, within its limits. Continue with NICE and persistence as a reference, BLEND, solar cycles and timestamps and the research thread.
