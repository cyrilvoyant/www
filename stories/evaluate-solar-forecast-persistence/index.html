How to evaluate a solar forecast against persistence

Compare the model with persistence (the forecast that repeats the last observed value) on the same targets, horizon (the delay between the last known value and the time forecast) and scope, report more than one error norm, then test the difference.

The ratio of the model's error to that of persistence says what the model adds. The NICEᵏ metrics (Voyant et al., 2025) build this ratio for norms of order one, two and three (mean absolute error, root mean squared error, and its cubic counterpart).

Cyril Voyant · Mines Paris – PSL, Centre O.I.E. · 2 November 2026

In brief

  • A forecast is judged against a named reference: simple persistence, smart persistence or BLEND.
  • Same model, different reference: in Ajaccio, AR(2) scores about +0.41 against simple persistence, but +0.03 against smart persistence.
  • Report several error norms on the same targets, then test the difference; the NICE metrics divide each norm by that of persistence.

Reading the quantities.

  • MAE is mean absolute error; RMSE is root mean squared error and gives large errors more weight. nRMSE normalizes RMSE by the mean observation on the evaluated data.
  • AR is autoregression; ELM (extreme learning machine) fits output weights while keeping a fixed random hidden layer.
  • In the NICE formulas, the subscript p denotes persistence; the statistical p-value reported by a test is a different quantity.
  • sCV (stochastic coefficient of variation) measures how far irradiance departs from the clear-sky curve: 0 under a clear sky, 1 for a sky that alternates at random between clear sky and thick cloud.
  • F (forecastability) lies between 1 − sCV, when those departures keep no memory, and 1, when they are perfectly correlated in time; unlike sCV, it depends on the horizon (the delay between the last known value and the time forecast).
  • Both are explained, with an interactive page, in Which forecast for which signal?; the study is summarized in sCV and F, explained; equations and code are on the methods page.

The steps

  1. Name the reference. Say which persistence is used: last observation, clear-sky-index persistence (it repeats the last clear-sky index: irradiance divided by clear-sky irradiance), or a phase-aware reference such as BLEND (a convex combination of persistence and cyclic persistence, the value one cycle earlier). A skill score (the gain over the reference) means little without this.
  2. Use the same targets. Same site, period, horizon and forecast times for the model and the reference.
  3. State the scope. Daytime only, or day and night. Including night changes the evaluated population and the normalization.
  4. Report several norms. MAE and RMSE do not weight large errors alike. Keep absolute errors next to relative scores.
  5. Test the difference. Compare error distributions across stations or periods, for instance with a Wilcoxon test (it compares two series of errors through their ranks).
  6. Describe the signal. A large error does not mean the same thing under a regular sky and under strongly fluctuating irradiance. sCV and F characterize variability and forecastability before algorithms are compared.

Same model, different reference: a worked example

The published table of the solar forecasting course gives, for the same test year, the error of five forecasts. Changing the reference or the scope changes the score of one and the same model: this is why steps 1 and 3 matter.

With night hours, the nRMSE of AR(2) goes from 21.3 % to 30.1 %: its RMSE falls (from 79 to 56 W/m²; at night every forecast is right), but the mean observation is halved (from 372 to 186 W/m²). Against the same reference, the score barely moves. Hence: name the reference, and state the scope.

NICE, a worked case

NICE¹ = MAE / MAEp and NICE² = RMSE / RMSEp, where p is simple persistence at the same horizon. NICE³ uses the cube root of the mean cubed absolute error. NICEΣ combines the three.

A useful predictor lies between 0 and 1. Above 1, the model does worse than the simplest predictor, or there is a numerical or other problem.

In the published study, at a thirty-minute horizon, an ELM and an autoregressive model share a rounded median nRMSE of 0.19. NICEΣ gives 0.75 versus 0.77. On that protocol the test gives p < 0.001 for NICEΣ and p = 0.0591 for nRMSE (p: under the test’s null hypothesis, the probability of a statistic at least as extreme as the observed one). Validation combines Monte Carlo simulations and 68 SIAR stations in Spain (the Spanish agro-climatic network).

Limits

These values concern that protocol and those distributions. They do not measure the economic value of an improvement, and they do not rank methods in general.
