How I choose a model

I build models, and the tools that make them usable, to inform decisions in energy and medicine: helping an engineer size a system, an operator anticipate production, a physicist interpret a dose. I start from the problem: what is known about the system, the data available and the precision really needed. I use an explicit model when it fits, complete it with learning when a relation is missing, and keep extra complexity only when its benefit is demonstrated. AI is fully part of the toolbox; evaluation and responsibility stay human.

My research in 30 seconds · A short brief for the research thread, selected work and a demonstration.

I am a physicist and Research Director at Mines Paris – PSL, within the Centre Observation, Impacts, Energy (O.I.E.), and Visiting Professor at the University of Kragujevac, Serbia. I build physical and statistical models to inform decisions in energy and medicine, with people at the center and AI as one tool among others. Our work connects solar and photovoltaic forecasting, time series and frugal AI. My background also includes medical physics.

Can we know how predictable the sky is? When does a simple model beat a complex one? Which forecast errors matter in practice? Here I explain the results behind these questions, with the papers and code that support them.

One method, four complementary routes

The domain changes; the method does not. The route is chosen by the problem and the decision to inform, not by the tool; extra complexity is kept only when its benefit is demonstrated. These four routes lead to the five-step thread: the fifth step, showing why the result can be trusted, applies to each of them.

Compute directly

When the link is known, compute it with an explicit model, calibrated and validated on data; learning is added only for a demonstrated benefit.

Which forecast for which signal? · Atmospheric neutrons and BNCT: quantify the limit · Compute, don't learn: radiobiological dose equivalence

Hybridize

When the model falls short, complete it: keep the model, learn only what is missing.

Thyroid cancer and radioiodine: model first · Better persistence, without training a neural network

Learn frugally

When the useful relations are not known well enough, evaluate what the data can teach, with only as much complexity as pays.

Seven signals, one frugal learner (ELM) · Satellite forecasting: one cell or the whole field? · Frugal models for solar forecasting (in French)

AI in its place

Language models help people use validated tools; the calculation, the checking and the responsibility stay with validated codes and people.

Let the language model read, let the validated code compute

Demonstrate trust

Judge against a reference, publish code, data and limits, make research findable and reusable.

Forecasting the sun with its uncertainty: ELM against persistence, demonstration over three days

At every moment, two models forecast the next four hours of a photovoltaic plant in Palaiseau: persistence, which copies the last observed value, and the ELM, a frugal neural network. Each band is a range that should contain the observation. The bands target nominal coverages of 50% and 90% (the share of observations they should contain); their achieved coverage must be measured. The black curve shows observations.

Reading the animation: glossary
Deterministic forecast ŷ
A single forecast value, the solid line of each model.
Prediction interval
A range [lower bound, upper bound] that should contain the observation with a stated probability: the coloured bands.
Nominal coverage (1 − α)
The target probability: 50% for the dark band, 90% for the light band (α = 0.1).
σ, residual standard deviation
The model’s typical error, measured on the training period, one value per model and horizon.
Factor k and lookup table (LUT)
For each target coverage, the table gives the multiple of σ to use: interval = ŷ ± k·σ. It is built once, on training data, without assuming Gaussian errors (for Gaussian errors, k ≈ 1.64 at 90%).
Lower bound ≥ 0
A plant’s power is zero or positive: a bound below zero has no physical meaning, so it is raised to 0.
PICP (Prediction Interval Coverage Probability)
Observed coverage: the share of observations falling inside the interval. A 90% interval is reliable if its PICP is close to 90%.
MIL (Mean Interval Length)
Mean interval width, in kW. At equal coverage, narrower is more useful: this is sharpness.
Interval score
Interval width plus a penalty of 2/α = 20 times the miss when the observation falls outside: it rewards both reliability and sharpness; lower is better.
Persistence
The simplest reference: the near future looks like now, the forecast is the last observed value.
ELM (Extreme Learning Machine)
A one-hidden-layer neural network whose input weights are drawn at random and only output weights are learned, by regularized least squares: fast and frugal.

Scores accumulate over revealed daytime targets, pooling horizons from 30 min to 4 h; one observation contributes at several horizons.

Public Palaiseau data (30-min steps) and the ridge ELM of the PV_ELM_pred repository; simple persistence: the last observed value. Both models use the Appendix C method: an empirical table maps target coverage to factor k, learned from training residuals, then the interval is forecast ± kσ. One table and residual standard deviation per model and horizon. Three days were selected for their variability: this is not a representative test or a manuscript result. Software: Martin Leiva, Xavier Silvani, Gilles Notton, Cyril Voyant and Alan Julien. Cite the software and its team.

Lookup table, method and reference

Method applied here: Cyril Voyant, Milan Despotovic, Gilles Notton, Yves-Marie Saint-Drenan, Mohammed Asloune, Luis Garcia-Gutierrez, On the Importance of Clearsky Model in Short-Term Solar Radiation Forecasting (2025), Appendix C. Symmetric intervals ŷ ± kα σ are calibrated from training residuals, then applied to later forecasts. The Palaiseau table is recalculated for this PV application; these are not Figure C.9 values or the paper’s SIAR results.

Reading the table. For coverage c = 1 − α, k(c) is the empirical quantile of |y − ŷ|/σ on training data. The residual standard deviation σ uses n − 1 in its denominator. Model and table use the first half of the series; the three displayed days are in the last quarter. Interpolate k between tabulated coverages (1% steps). Only addition to Appendix C: the lower bound is raised to 0, since a photovoltaic plant never produces negative power. As the observation is always positive, coverage (PICP) is unchanged; only the mean width (MIL) shrinks. Nominal coverage is not a guarantee out of sample.

The interval score is IS = (U − L) + (2/α) max(L − y, 0) + (2/α) max(y − U, 0), with α = 0.10. It complements PICP and MIL by penalising observations outside the band. It is not the CRPS in Appendix D.

About

Combining medical physics in radiotherapy (2003–2024) and energy-forecasting research (PhD in 2011), he works on solar and energy forecasting, forecast evaluation, frugal AI and language-model interfaces to scientific codes.
