AI in research: the AI Act, a lab charter and a toolbox for researchers

Step of the story · Demonstrate trustThe approach

Judge against a reference, publish code, data and limits, make research findable and reusable. Principle 5: Trust is demonstrated.

Generative AI now writes code, drafts papers and reviews data in every laboratory. The European AI Act sets rules for what we build and what we transfer. Here is what changes for researchers, the timeline in Europe and in France, how the rest of the world compares, who is responsible when research code becomes a product, and a toolbox a lab can adopt in a few hours per project.

Cyril Voyant, Mines Paris – PSL, Centre O.I.E. · status on 23 October 2026 · not legal advice: check with your institution's legal service

In brief

  • Two research exclusions, not one: systems developed and put into service for the sole purpose of scientific research (art. 2(6)), and research, testing and development before placing on the market, except testing in real-world conditions (art. 2(8)). The rules start when a model is placed on the market or put into service, for example when a partner deploys it in an energy system. It is high-risk when it serves as a safety component in the supply of electricity, gas or heating (Annex III, point 2); otherwise its class must be assessed case by case.
  • The deadlines moved in 2026: transparency rules apply since 2 August 2026, high-risk systems of Annex III from 2 December 2027, AI in regulated products from 2 August 2028 (Regulation (EU) 2026/1744).
  • A lab does not need a compliance department: a one-page charter, a data card and a model card, a minimal test plan and six extra fields in the data management plan give a project a documentation base that an audit can examine. The durations given below are indicative; none of this guarantees compliance.

What AI changes in research

AI speeds up what used to take days: writing code, summarizing literature, cleaning data, drafting text. It also creates new risks for science itself: invented references, fluent but wrong code, papers produced at industrial scale, models nobody can reproduce, and results that depend on a tool whose version changes without notice. The answer is the same as for any scientific tool: say what was used, check it, document it, and make it reproducible. Trust is not claimed, it is demonstrated (see questioning how we do research).

The AI Act in plain words

  • One logic: the risk of the use. Some uses are banned (manipulation, social scoring), some are high-risk and strictly regulated (safety components of critical infrastructure such as electricity, gas or heating supply, medical devices, education, employment), some carry transparency duties (chatbots, synthetic content), and the rest is free.
  • Two main roles. The provider develops a system and places it on the market or puts it into service under its own name (art. 3(3), 16). The deployer uses it under its authority (art. 26). Anyone who puts their name on a system, substantially modifies it or changes its purpose can become the provider (art. 25).
  • Research is excluded, under conditions. Systems and models developed and put into service for the sole purpose of scientific research are outside the Act (art. 2(6)), and so are research, testing and development before placing on the market, except tests in real-world conditions (art. 2(8)). Free and open-source components are largely exempt unless they are high-risk, banned, or subject to transparency duties (art. 2(12)).
  • Generative AI. The obligations of August 2025 bind the providers of general-purpose models (OpenAI, Mistral, Google...), not the researchers who use them. Labeling synthetic content (art. 50) mainly binds providers and, for published deepfakes or texts informing the public without human editorial control, deployers. Declaring AI use in a paper or a thesis is required by publishers, funders and research-integrity rules (no AI as an author, disclosure of use), more than by the Act itself.
  • AI literacy (art. 4). Organizations that provide or deploy AI systems take measures so that their staff understand them; the 2026 amendment softened this into a duty of means, with support from the Commission and Member States.

The timeline in Europe and in France

Date What applies
1 August 2024 The AI Act (Regulation (EU) 2024/1689) enters into force
2 February 2025 Banned practices; AI literacy
2 August 2025 Obligations of providers of general-purpose AI models; governance; penalties
27 July 2026 Digital omnibus on AI (Regulation (EU) 2026/1744) in force: high-risk deadlines postponed
2 August 2026 Transparency obligations (art. 50) and most other provisions; national market surveillance
2 December 2026 End of the grace period for marking synthetic content of systems already on the market (art. 50(2))
9 December 2026 New Product Liability Directive (EU) 2024/2853: software is a product
2 December 2027 High-risk systems of Annex III (safety components of critical infrastructure, education, employment...)
2 August 2028 High-risk AI embedded in regulated products (Annex I: machinery, medical devices...)

In France, a bill adapting national law to EU law (adopted by the Senate on 18 February 2026, examined by the National Assembly in autumn 2026) organizes supervision: the DGCCRF as coordinator and single point of contact, the CNIL for banned practices and part of the high-risk uses, Arcom for synthetic content, the ACPR for finance, with technical support from ANSSI and PEReN. Until the law is passed, the European rules apply directly; the designation of authorities is what remains to be settled.

Elsewhere: no equivalent of the AI Act, but rules everywhere

Region Approach (October 2026)
United States No federal AI law. A December 2025 executive order pushes for a national framework that would override state laws, but Congress has not passed it. State laws apply: California (frontier model transparency) and Texas since January 2026, Colorado from January 2027.
China Rules by use case: generative AI services (2023), algorithm filing, mandatory labeling of AI-generated content since 1 September 2025, enforced by the Cyberspace Administration.
South Korea The closest equivalent: a horizontal AI Basic Act in force since 22 January 2026 (high-impact AI, transparency, local representative for foreign firms), with a one-year grace period on fines.
Japan AI Promotion Act (2025): a framework to promote and coordinate, without penalties; guidelines for businesses.
Canada, Brazil Canada's federal AI bill lapsed in 2025; Brazil's bill, close to the European model, passed the Senate in December 2024 and was still before the Chamber at our last check.

What it means for a European lab: partners abroad work under different, often lighter rules, but the AI Act reaches any system whose output is used in the EU (art. 2(1)). A shared framework is what makes collaboration safe: without it, nobody knows who answers for a model once it leaves the lab.

Who is responsible when research code becomes a product?

A typical case: a forecasting model written in part by three laboratories, one in France, one elsewhere in the EU and one at an Asian partner, within a joint project. An industrial company reuses the code in an energy management system sold in Europe.

  • While the code serves research, the three labs are outside the AI Act. They remain answerable for scientific integrity, for the documentation they deliver and for the terms of the license and the consortium agreement.
  • When the company puts the system into service under its name, it becomes the provider: if the system is a safety component of the electricity supply, it is high-risk (Annex III, point 2, from 2 December 2027): risk management, technical file, conformity assessment and CE marking. The grid operator that uses it is the deployer: human oversight, monitoring, logs.
  • If the partner deploys the model in its own country, local law applies (for example the Korean Basic Act or the Chinese labeling rules); the AI Act applies only if the output is used in the EU.
  • Civil liability follows the new Product Liability Directive from 9 December 2026: software counts as a product, but free and open-source software supplied outside a commercial activity is excluded. Everything else is settled by contracts: who may reuse the code, for what purpose, with which warranties and which duty to inform.

The practical lesson: write the transfer into the contract before the code leaves the lab, and hand over a documented, tested model. A model without documentation leaves its new owner with a risk nobody can measure.

What a lab can do: the example of the O.I.E. framework

At the Centre O.I.E. (Mines Paris – PSL, about 30 people), a FAIR + AI framework has been drafted since 2025 and is being finalized, for deployment from 2027. Its main lines:

  • A one-page charter signed by every member: generative AI allowed as an assistant for writing, code and figures, with explicit mention of its use; human review of everything it produces; minimal documentation (data card, model card, versioned Git repository with README); every AI project in a register, reassessed when it moves from research to industry; a data management plan with six AI fields.
  • Light roles, about 0.3 full-time equivalent in total: an AI compliance lead (register, regulatory watch, partners), a FAIR data steward (data management plans, data cards, licences, GDPR) and an MLOps and quality referent (model cards, test plans, drift), plus an ethics committee of four people meeting each quarter for sensitive projects.
  • Six steps per project (indicative durations): register (5 minutes); data management plan with AI fields (1 hour); data card and model card (2 hours); minimal test plan (2 to 3 hours); README with the AI-use statement (30 minutes); for a transfer, an audit-ready file (one day).
  • Indicators: at least 95 % of projects documented, less than five days between project start and an enriched plan, partner satisfaction above 8/10.

At the institution level, Mines Paris – PSL's legal service has built, with the research centers, a self-assessment decision tree: who is the actor and in which role, the essential questions, the impacts, the qualification of the AI system in the risk pyramid, the conditions of use and of making it available. Its purpose is to find the tipping point where a project enters the scope of the AI Act, and to adapt the contract and its technical annex in time. It is a good example of what an institution can offer its researchers.

The toolbox

Each tool opens on a filled example, taken from the O.I.E. templates (values marked illustrative are not results). Download the nine templates as a kit (Markdown files in a zip, ready to fill in).

1. Situate the project. Research only, transfer, or deployment? Who will be provider and deployer? Which risk class? Real-world tests are not covered by the research exemption.

2. State the use of AI. In a paper, a thesis, a report or a README: which tool, which version, for what, checked by whom and when. Example: "Parts of the data-loading code were drafted with GitHub Copilot (2026); all functions were reviewed and unit-tested by A. Author on 12 March 2026."

Example: README with the AI-use statement

Project: MLP, global horizontal irradiance (GHI) forecasting · Author: First Last · Supervisor: First Last · O.I.E., Mines Paris – PSL

  • Purpose: train a multilayer perceptron to forecast hourly GHI from weather data.
  • Use of AI: occasional help from a generative AI assistant (name and version) for boilerplate code and debugging. Every suggestion was reviewed by the author (unit tests, validation by the supervisor), name and date.
  • Data: SIAR network (Spain), 2005–2015; normalization, gap handling; public, non-personal data; license stated.
  • Tests: fixed seed, identical results on repeated runs; cross-validation (k > 3 folds); stress test on irradiance peaks and missing values; drift checked with nRMSE and NICE on recent data.
  • Reuse: academic research; any industrial use must be agreed with the laboratory.

3. Keep a human in charge. No AI as an author; every reference, equation and line of code checked by a named person.

4. Document the data (data card). Origin, period, volume, variables and units, preprocessing, quality, known biases, license or legal basis, transfers outside the EU, conditions of reuse.

Example: data card (one page)

Field What to write Example
Dataset name Name used in the project SIAR_GHI_ES_2005_2015
Origin Provider or station network SIAR network (Spain)
Period, volume Dates covered and size 2005–2015, 10 stations, 50k records
Variables Main variables and units GHI (W/m²), temperature (°C), humidity (%)
Preprocessing Steps applied Min-max normalization, gap interpolation
Quality Quick assessment 3 % missing values, calibrated sensors
Known biases Points of attention Uneven geographical coverage
License, legal basis (GDPR) License or legal ground CC BY 4.0, public data
Transfers outside the EU Yes or no, and safeguards No (storage in the EU only)
Reuse Free, contract or restricted Free for academic purposes

5. Document the model (model card). Name and version, architecture, training and test data with the split, intended use, metrics against an explicit reference (persistence, for example with NICE metrics), limits, human oversight, link to the code version.

Subgroups Results by climate or season Weaker in Mediterranean climate

Reuse Free, license or contract Free for research; industrial use under agreement

6. Test before sharing. Reproducibility (fixed seed, same results), robustness (cross-validation, subsamples), stress tests (extreme or missing values), drift on recent data.

Example: minimal test plan (checklist, half a day)

Test How Why Who validates
Reproducibility Fixed seed, identical results; hash of weights and dataset A third party can reproduce the results PhD student and supervisor
Robustness k-fold cross-validation or subsamples The model does not overfit one dataset PhD student
Stress test Extreme data: irradiance peaks, gaps, sensor anomalies Stable behavior outside normal conditions Supervisor
Drift nRMSE and NICE on recent versus older data, with thresholds Anticipate loss of performance MLOps and quality referent
Licences Every dataset and code reused respects its license Legal compliance FAIR data steward
README and AI statement Use of generative AI stated Transparency PhD student
Final review Scientific and technical reading Quality before release Responsible supervisor

For a model in operation, a monitoring plan adds thresholds, for example: nRMSE checked monthly, alert above +0.05 and critical above +0.10, then analysis and retraining; data coverage checked weekly, alert below 95 %; more than three incidents a month triggers an urgent diagnosis.

7. Add six fields to the data management plan. Legal basis; human oversight; AI transparency; conditions of reuse; international transfers; impact assessment (DPIA where personal-data processing is likely to create a high risk to people, FRIA for some high-risk deployments).

Example: the six AI fields of a data management plan

  1. Legal basis: for personal data, identify the applicable basis under Article 6 GDPR, and Article 9 conditions where relevant. Record copyright licences and reuse agreements separately; they do not replace a GDPR legal basis.
  2. Human oversight: who validates the results, when, at which level (in, on or outside the loop).
  3. AI transparency: whether content or code was generated with AI, and how it is stated.
  4. Conditions of reuse: free, under license, or reserved to a contract.
  5. International transfers: whether data leave the EU (non-European cloud, partner abroad) and the safeguards.
  6. Impact assessment: DPIA where personal-data processing is likely to create a high risk to people; FRIA for some high-risk public deployments; otherwise, why neither applies.

Example: DPIA and FRIA screening, two contrasting projects

Document the decision and its justification; a numerical score does not determine a legal obligation. DPIA: the controller assesses whether personal-data processing is likely to pose a high risk, with advice from the DPO where designated (CNIL). FRIA: check the intended use, high-risk classification and deployer against Article 27; Annex III point 2 is excluded from this specific obligation.

  • Weather-only forecasting for a thesis: in this stated scenario, no personal-data processing requiring a DPIA is identified. Record the research context and check applicability again before deployment.
  • Household load forecasting for a grid operator: assess the privacy risks of consumption profiles with the controller and DPO. Do not infer a FRIA obligation from the energy sector alone. Document the intended use, applicable category and responsible parties; an assessment can also be undertaken voluntarily.

8. Keep a register of AI projects, uses and partners, and reassess when a project changes purpose.

Example: one line of the AI register

Project Model Use of generative AI Status Partners Data Last review
GHI forecasting, thesis 2026 ELM, MLP Code assistant, stated in README Research only None Public weather data Project start; on any change of purpose

9. Before any transfer, write the contract and prepare the file. Who becomes provider; license and intellectual property; warranties and liability; duty to report incidents; data transfers; the technical file (README, data card, model card, test plan and results, change log, monitoring plan).

Example: content of an audit-ready file

  • README with the use of generative AI and the licences of data and code;
  • data card (origin, biases, legal basis, reuse) and model card (type, data, metrics, limits, human oversight, Git traceability);
  • test plan and its results, validated by the supervisor;
  • data management plan with the six AI fields;
  • change log: versions of the model, Git commits, changes of the dataset;
  • for a high-risk deployment: monitoring plan (nRMSE, NICE, drift, thresholds) and an incident procedure (reporting channel, named person, time to fix).

10. Train everyone. A short session for PhD students and staff each year covers AI literacy and the lab's charter.

Example: a one-page laboratory charter

As a member of the laboratory, I commit to the following principles for using and developing artificial intelligence, in line with the AI Act and the FAIR principles.

  1. Generative AI (chat assistants, code assistants) is allowed as an aid for writing, code and figures, and its use is always stated (report, paper, README).
  2. Human review: everything produced with AI is checked by a person; technical and bibliographic review before any release. The author remains responsible.
  3. Minimal documentation for every project: a data card, a model card, a versioned Git repository with a README (the institution's platform; projects may stay private).
  4. AI register: every project using AI is listed; moving from research to industry triggers a new assessment.
  5. Data management plan with the six AI fields: legal basis, human oversight, AI transparency, reuse, international transfers, impact assessment.

Name, role, date, signature.

Glossary

GDPR

General Data Protection Regulation, Regulation (EU) 2016/679: the European law on personal data. See the CNIL guide.

DPIA

Data Protection Impact Assessment (art. 35 GDPR): required when processing personal data is likely to result in a high risk for people. CNIL method and tool.

FRIA

Fundamental Rights Impact Assessment (art. 27 AI Act): required from some deployers of high-risk AI systems, mainly public bodies and private entities providing public services, before first use.

MLOps

Machine-learning operations: the practices that keep a model reproducible and reliable once in use: versioning of data and code, automated tests, monitoring of performance and drift, controlled retraining.

Data management plan (DMP)

A document describing how a project collects, documents, stores, shares and archives its data, required by ANR and Horizon Europe; written in France with DMP OPIDoR.

Data card, model card

One- to two-page sheets that describe a dataset or a trained model: origin, content, intended use, metrics, limits. The format follows the "model cards" and "datasheets for datasets" proposals of the machine-learning community.

Frequently asked questions

Is scientific research exempt from the AI Act?

The exclusions depend on the system's purpose and stage, not simply on the user's status as a researcher. Article 2 covers systems specifically developed and put into service solely for scientific research, and certain pre-market research activities. Real-world testing is outside the latter exclusion. Reassess the scope before transfer or deployment.

Official source

Which high-risk AI deadlines should a laboratory track?

As checked on October 23, 2026, the revised timeline provides December 2, 2027 for Annex III high-risk uses and August 2, 2028 for high-risk AI embedded in regulated products under Annex I. Other obligations have their own dates. Identify the relevant system and provision before applying a deadline.

Official source

Who is responsible when laboratory code is transferred?

Identify the provider, deployer and other actors for the actual system. For high-risk systems, Article 25 specifies circumstances in which rebranding, substantial modification or a changed intended purpose can make another actor the provider. Contracts should document roles; they do not replace the applicable statutory obligations.

Official source

What should a laboratory document first?

Record the purpose, people responsible, data provenance, model and code versions, validation results and limits. Use data and model cards, an AI-use statement and a project register. Before a transfer, review roles and documentation with the institution's legal service. These are practical recommendations, not a compliance certification.
