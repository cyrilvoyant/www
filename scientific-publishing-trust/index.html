Scientific publishing: no verification, no trust

How can verification become as visible as publication? The number of indexed articles grew by about 47% between 2016 and 2022 [1], peer review relies on unpaid work, and Artificial intelligence that produces text, images or code from a prompt. makes plausible text almost free. The answer is not more journals: it is to check the data and code behind what is published, with the same rigor Europe now demands of AI systems.

Overview: questioning how we do research →

In short

  • The picture. About 47% more indexed articles from 2016 to 2022 [1], more than 100 million hours of unpaid peer review in 2020 [4], more than 10,000 retractions in 2023 [5].
  • What exists opens access. Findable, Accessible, Interoperable, Reusable: principles for data, not a license. principles, open access, the right to deposit: almost no text asks anyone to check that a published result can be reproduced from its data and code.
  • The point of comparison. The Regulation (EU) 2024/1689 on artificial intelligence: obligations depend on the risk level of the AI system. requires of high-risk AI systems what articles lack: data governance, documentation, logs, quality, post-market monitoring.
  • What is missing. Checking data and code, declaring tools (AI included), following the article after publication, recognizing editorial and review work.

From publication to checkable evidence

Reproducing a number does not prove that the model describes the world correctly. These levels answer different questions: documenting them separately avoids turning a badge or a repository into a blanket guarantee.

Connect practices without confusing their purposes

Read

FAIR (findable, accessible, interoperable, reusable) supports discovery and reuse; licenses define permissions; reproducibility lets others check a computation; the AI Act (the EU regulation on AI) regulates specified AI uses. RTVACOS (rigour, transparency, visibility, adoption, collaboration, outreach, societal relevance) connects this work to research excellence. These frameworks complement rather than replace one another.

The global picture: too many articles, too little verification

  • Output that outruns researchers. Articles indexed in Scopus and Web of Science rose by about 47% from 2016 to 2022, faster than the number of scientists; part of the growth came from special issues with shortened review times [1].
  • A concentrated market. Five publishers published more than half of all papers as early as 2013 [2]; the group that owns the largest reported an adjusted operating margin of 34.8% in 2025 [3].
  • Invisible work. Reviewers gave more than 100 million hours in 2020 [4], unpaid and almost unrecognized.
  • Eroded trust. More than 10,000 papers were retracted in 2023, over 8,000 of them in one publisher's journals, mostly in special issues exploited by Organizations selling fabricated or falsified articles, often slipped into special issues. [5].
  • AI banned rather than framed. Rules say what not to do, for instance the ban on generative AI in US grant review [6], rarely what to show.

Step by step: who checks what in an article's life?

Pick a step. For each one: what exists, who checks, what remains uncovered. Everything comes from this page's sources and the table above; arrow keys move between steps.

1Write, with tools (AI included)

What exists

  • Rules that mostly say what not to do: for instance, generative AI is banned from US grant review [6].
  • For comparison, the AI Act requires technical documentation and record-keeping of high-risk AI systems (Art. 11 and 12), not of articles [16].

Who checks?

Partly the journal's reviewers and editor; no mechanism in the table is designed for articles.

What remains uncovered

Declaring the tools used, AI included: neither ban nor silence, a statement of what was used, for what, and what a human checked.

5Reuse the data and code

What exists

  • FAIR principles: data that are findable, accessible, interoperable and reusable [8]; principles, not a license.
  • Licenses: they define reuse permissions.
  • Software Heritage archives the code [19]; SoftwareX publishes software articles and credits developers [20].

The repository and data curators; for licenses, no one: the author declares.

The AI Act as a comparison, not a regulation of papers

Read

The AI Act requires providers of high-risk AI systems to govern their training, validation and test data (Art. 10), keep technical documentation (Art. 11), log events (Art. 12), run a quality management system (Art. 17) and monitor the system after it is placed on the market (Art. 72) [16]. These obligations overlap with some Machine Learning Operations: engineering practices to version, trace, deploy and monitor learning models. practices (running and monitoring machine-learning models in production), without being equivalent. Scientific articles follow different editorial, scientific and sometimes regulatory rules; independent execution is not automatically guaranteed.

The regulation also excludes AI developed solely for research (Art. 2(6)). A gap may open: European teams bear the cost of compliance as soon as they deploy, teams outside the regulation do not. The AI Act remains essential all the same: it provides one framework for traceability from data to result. The right answer is not to weaken it but to bring its spirit to publishing.

France: editorial work that does not count

Read

France has one of the most open-access-friendly legal frameworks in Europe: a mandatory right to deposit, HAL, Software Heritage, a national plan that covers source code [17] [18]. Yet editing a journal, reviewing or checking code is almost absent from career assessment, which counts articles first. The work that guarantees quality is the work that pays least; editors and reviewers become hard to find, and others end up running the reference journals.

What is missing

  • Checking the data and code of every article that depends on them, with a traceability requirement comparable to what the AI Act asks of AI systems: frozen versions, documented environment, independent execution, kept records.
  • Declaring the tools used, AI included. Neither ban nor silence: a statement of what was used, for what, and what a human checked.
  • Following the article after publication, as one monitors a deployed system: versions, errata, reports, links to corrected datasets.
  • Recognizing editorial and review work in assessment, as CoARA proposes.
  • Connecting the frameworks: code licenses, FAIR data and the AI Act talk about the same thing, traceability, but do not cite each other.

This is not necessarily about more journals, but about changing what we ask of the existing ones. On this site, every study links to its code and data; the page AI in research shows how to declare tools, the page Data how to choose a license, the page Cite and reuse how to give credit.

No verification, no trust

An article no one can redo is a claim, not a result. The tools to check exist, AI included; what is missing is making them normal, and recognizing the people who do that work.

Questions and answers

Is scientific publishing really in trouble?

The number of indexed articles grew by about 47% between 2016 and 2022, faster than the number of researchers; unpaid peer review no longer keeps up, and more than 10,000 papers were retracted in 2023, a record.

Should AI be banned from writing and reviewing?

No: it should be declared. As with any other tool, authors state what was used and for what; what matters is that results are checked, data and code included.

Why bring the AI Act into a discussion of articles?

Because it requires of high-risk AI systems what articles lack: data governance, technical documentation, record-keeping, a quality management system and post-market monitoring.

Do we need new journals?

Not necessarily. What is missing is verification and recognition of editorial work, not more titles.
