NICE metrics: when RMSE hides a difference between solar forecasts

When RMSE hides a difference

Step 5 of 5 · Demonstrate trustSee it on the thread

Judge against a reference, publish code, data and limits, make research findable and reusable. Trust is demonstrated.

Cyril Voyant · 18 October 2026

At a thirty-minute horizon (the delay between the last measurement and the forecast time), the NICE study reports a rounded median nRMSE (the RMSE divided by the mean of the signal) of 0.19 for both an ELM (extreme learning machine, a small network with a random hidden layer) and an autoregressive model (which forecasts from the latest values). The composite NICEΣ score, which compares the model’s error with that of persistence (the forecast that repeats the last measurement; 0 is perfect, 1 is as much error as persistence), nevertheless gives 0.75 versus 0.77, with a statistically significant difference in the reported test. The finding is more than a change of notation: the evaluation metric changes how effectively forecasts can be distinguished.

The NICEᵏ metrics were introduced by Voyant et al. in Sustainable Energy Technologies and Assessments (2025).

Published in Sustainable Energy Technologies and Assessments, the study introduces a family based on error norms of orders one, two and three (one: mean absolute error, MAE; two: RMSE; three: cube root of the mean cubed absolute error). NICE¹ tracks average errors, NICE² gives more weight to large deviations and NICE³ amplifies extremes. NICEΣ combines those dimensions. A persistence reference places error in the context of the series, rather than normalizing solely by mean irradiance. Each component is the model’s error divided by that of simple persistence at the same horizon: 0 for a perfect forecast, 1 for persistence itself, and above 1 when persistence does better. The components therefore describe different aspects of performance instead of assuming that a single conventional error statistic answers every evaluation question.

Validation combines Monte Carlo simulations (repeated random draws) with observations from 68 SIAR stations (the agro-climatic network) in Spain. Comparisons include autoregressive models (AR), the extreme learning machine (EL: a random hidden layer whose output weights are fitted analytically), smart persistence (SP: persistence of the clear-sky index) and ARTU (a second-order reference on the ratio to trend). The paper distinguishes a theoretical construction, which depends on strict statistical assumptions, from empirical evaluation. It does not justify substituting a theoretical expression for observed-error calculations without checking those assumptions.

In the thirty-minute example, the tests give p < 0.001 for NICEΣ and p = 0.0591 for nRMSE (p is the p-value of the test: the probability of a difference at least as large if the two models performed equally). These values concern that protocol and those distributions; they do not directly measure the economic importance of the improvement. Users can select sensitivity to extreme errors that fits their application while retaining absolute errors and calculation assumptions. The public PV_ELM_pred code documents the components and their average. Relative scores above one should remain visible: persistence can outperform the model being evaluated.
