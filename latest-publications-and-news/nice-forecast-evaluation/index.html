NICE metrics: when RMSE hides a difference between solar forecasts

When RMSE hides a difference

Step of the story · Demonstrate trustThe approach

Judge against a reference, publish code, data and limits, make research findable and reusable. Principle 5: Trust is demonstrated.

Cyril Voyant · 18 October 2026

At a thirty-minute horizon, the NICE study reports a rounded median nRMSE of 0.19 for both an ELM and an autoregressive model. The composite NICEΣ score nevertheless gives 0.75 versus 0.77, with a statistically significant difference in the reported test. The finding is more than a change of notation: the evaluation metric changes how effectively forecasts can be distinguished.

Published in Sustainable Energy Technologies and Assessments, the study introduces a family based on error norms of orders one, two and three. NICE¹ tracks average errors, NICE² gives more weight to large deviations and NICE³ amplifies extremes. NICEΣ combines those dimensions. A persistence reference places error in the context of the series, rather than normalizing solely by mean irradiance. The components therefore describe different aspects of performance instead of assuming that a single conventional error statistic answers every evaluation question.

Validation combines Monte Carlo simulations with observations from 68 SIAR stations in Spain. Comparisons include autoregressive models, Extreme Learning and smart persistence. The paper distinguishes a theoretical construction, which depends on strict statistical assumptions, from empirical evaluation. It does not justify substituting a theoretical expression for observed-error calculations without checking those assumptions.

In the thirty-minute example, the tests give p < 0.001 for NICEΣ and p = 0.0591 for nRMSE. These values concern that protocol and those distributions; they do not directly measure the economic importance of the improvement. Users can select sensitivity to extreme errors that fits their application while retaining absolute errors and calculation assumptions. The public PV_ELM_pred code documents the components and their average. Relative scores above one should remain visible: persistence can outperform the model being evaluated.
