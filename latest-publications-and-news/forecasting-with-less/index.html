BLEND: a better persistence forecast without training a neural network

Better persistence

Step of the story · HybridizeThe approach

When the model falls short, complete it: keep the model, learn only what is missing. Principle 2: The model at the core.

Cyril Voyant · 18 October 2026

Accounting for daily phase improves persistence forecasts on the solar series studied, with differences widening at longer lead times. In our Applied Mathematical Modeling paper, BLEND supplies this baseline directly from raw irradiance, without normalizing observations by an external clear-sky model.

The starting point is physical and statistical: irradiance does not have the same statistical properties throughout the day. Part of their evolution repeats with the cycle. Simply carrying the latest observation forward misses that structure. BLEND operators combine immediate memory with phase-aligned recurrence. The weight between the two is set for each phase of the cycle. Its MSE-optimal value, written with cyclic means, deviations and correlations, is close to a one-line least-squares fit on past cycles. The simplified coefficient λ = (1 + ρ)/2, also set for each phase from the local correlation ρ, is an approximation under the correlation assumptions of Remark 17, whose performance depends on the horizon. The resulting forecast has an explicit analytical construction rather than a hidden layer fitted to predict the target.

Validation combines controlled simulations with measurements from 68 Spanish stations. It compares persistence variants with more elaborate reference methods. On measured data, cyclostationary adjustments reduce errors relative to their naive counterparts; the paper also evaluates inter-model differences with Wilcoxon tests. Error distributions also reveal how performance varies across stations and forecast horizons.

The practical contribution is an interpretable, inexpensive analytical baseline against which to assess a trained model. Weights are estimated from historical observations in closed form, without iterative optimization. Cyclic statistics and correlations therefore require appropriate estimation. BLEND does not outperform every optimized method; autoregressive or learned predictors can do better under some conditions. Public MATLAB code makes the protocol inspectable and supports testing whether this simplicity transfers to another series. That test is more informative than assuming that additional modeling complexity must improve the forecast. The paper’s evidence concerns the studied datasets, rather than a universal ranking of forecasting methods.

Open code v1.1.1: an out-of-sample benchmark

The corrected MATLAB benchmark separates 6,000 observations for estimation from 6,000 for evaluation, with common forecast targets. It compares 21 references over five horizons and 10 synthetic realizations. The figure and table below report day-and-night nRMSE, normalized by the mean of the evaluated observations.

BLEND least squares per phase reaches mean nRMSE 0.3020, 0.3975, 0.4447, 0.4880 and 0.4954 at 1, 2, 3, 6 and 9 steps, compared with 0.5021 for cyclic persistence. Its weight is estimated by least squares in closed form, without iterative optimization. These estimates are not identical to the finite-sample moments estimator in general.

CLIPER on the clear-sky index has the lowest mean errors here. This is consistent with a synthetic signal constructed as clear sky multiplied by correlated noise. CLIPER on climatological anomalies also performs well without a clear-sky reference. Ten realizations of one generator do not establish a universal ranking or reproduce the paper’s SIAR experiments.
