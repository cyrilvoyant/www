sCV and F: how variable and how forecastable is solar irradiance

How forecastable is the sun?

Step 1 of 5 · Compute directlySee it on the thread

When the link is known, compute it with an explicit model, calibrated and validated on data; learning is added only for a demonstrated benefit. The model at the core.

Cyril Voyant · 18 October 2026

Across 68 Spanish weather stations, our forecastability indicator F (dimensionless: the closer to 1, the more usable memory the signal keeps) retains an inverse relationship with forecast error, examined across ten models and 24 horizons (the delay between the last measurement and the forecast time). This result, published in Renewable Energy, supports characterizing the signal before comparing algorithms: a large error means something different for a regular sky than for a strongly fluctuating irradiance series.

The sCV and F indicators were introduced by Voyant et al. in Renewable Energy (2026).

A useful diagnosis can rely on sufficient tools: complexity is added only when its benefit is demonstrated.

The study introduces two complementary indicators. The stochastic coefficient of variation, sCV, measures fluctuations relative to a dynamic clear-sky reference (the irradiance a cloudless sky would give). sCV equals 0 under clear sky; 1 is a reference level, not an upper bound. It corresponds to the theoretical case in which the signal switches randomly between clear sky and the diffuse floor (the weak scattered light left under thick cloud). sCV describes signal variability, not a forecast model’s performance against persistence (the forecast that repeats the last measurement). A value outside the expected domain calls for checking the data, preprocessing and normalization assumptions. F adds information about temporal dependence through autocorrelation (the correlation of the signal with its own past). F lies between 1 − sCV, when the departures from clear sky keep no memory, and 1, when they are perfectly correlated in time; unlike sCV, it depends on the forecast horizon. Two series with similar variability can therefore retain different amounts of memory, leaving different amounts of useful information for prediction.

Validation combines one hundred synthetic cyclostationary series (whose statistics repeat with the daily cycle) with observations from the SIAR network (the agro-climatic weather stations of Spain). Simulations allow controlled changes to signal properties; weather stations test the diagnosis against measured conditions. The association between F and forecast errors also appears at individual stations, rather than being solely a consequence of network aggregation. Under extreme synthetic variability, that association can become nonlinear.

For an operator or researcher, the contribution is diagnostic: compare forecasting difficulty, select meaningful baselines and interpret a score in context. It can inform a discussion of forecast risk before choosing a more elaborate model. F is neither a calibrated confidence interval nor a promise of financial savings. Overirradiance events (moments when cloud edges push irradiance above its clear-sky value) and the quality of the clear-sky reference also require attention. The study measures aspects of inherent forecastability; it does not remove atmospheric uncertainty or establish an operational benefit for every energy system. Those benefits require a separate decision-based evaluation.
