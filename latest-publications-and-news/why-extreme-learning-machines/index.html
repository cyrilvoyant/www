MIMO-ELM: seven energy signals of Corsica forecast by one model

Seven signals, one ELM

Step 3 of 5 · Learn frugallySee it on the thread

When the useful relations are not known well enough, evaluate what the data can teach.

Cyril Voyant · 18 October 2026

At a one-hour horizon (the delay between the last measurement and the forecast time), our MIMO-ELM model (one multiple-input multiple-output model, built on an extreme learning machine, that forecasts several signals together) achieves nRMSE values (the RMSE divided by the mean of the signal; 0 is a perfect forecast) of 0.179 for solar generation and 0.051 for thermal generation, with R² (the share of the variance of the series that the forecast explains; 1 is perfect) above 0.98 for both series. Total-demand forecasts remain reliable up to five hours ahead within the study. Published in Applied Energy, these results come from six years of hourly data for the Corsican electricity system.

The model handles seven variables: total demand, solar, wind, hydropower, thermal generation, bioenergy and imports. Its multiple-input multiple-output (MIMO) architecture, in which one model reads the recent history of all seven signals and forecasts them together, uses sliding windows (the latest values of each signal) and cyclic encodings (which place 11 pm next to midnight) to represent memory and seasonal variation. The Extreme Learning Machine retains a random hidden layer and fits its output coefficients analytically, avoiding iterative deep-network training.

Evaluation goes beyond an isolated score. The study compares the model with persistence (the last measurement repeated), single-output (SISO) formulations with one model per signal, and an LSTM (long short-term memory, a recurrent deep network) under a realistic tuning budget (a limited effort spent choosing the model settings). It reports improved skill (error reduction relative to a reference) against the reference methods and small but stable gains over SISO formulations. Wind and bioenergy remain more difficult because of their strong variability. These differences are useful findings in their own right: a shared architecture does not make every energy component equally predictable.

For an energy system, the contribution is a common, rapidly fitted framework for several components. It supports joint investigation of production and consumption. Multiple outputs alone, however, do not impose an exact physical energy balance; enforcing conservation requires an explicit constraint or reconciliation procedure. The reported nRMSE values are not percentage improvements over a competitor. They describe the Corsican evaluation protocol. Transfer to another region should be assessed using that region’s data, constraints and forecast horizons, rather than inferred from architecture alone.
