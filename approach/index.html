Understand before you compute: my approach

I start with the problem to solve. If known physical or mathematical relationships are sufficient, I use them directly. Otherwise, I use data to supplement the model or to build a forecast. I keep a more complex method only when its gain is demonstrated. AI (artificial intelligence) is one possible tool; verification and responsibility remain human.

Cyril Voyant · Research Director, Mines Paris – PSL, Centre O.I.E.

I often sum it up with a simple picture: three nested circles. At the center, the people who ask the question and take the decision; around them, a model we understand; on the outside, the tools, AI included, called on only when they help.

  • Radiobiology: compute dose equivalence under the assumptions of the chosen model, then state its limits.
  • Solar forecasting: compare every model with persistence (repeating the last observed value), on the same data and at the same horizon (how far ahead the forecast looks).
  • AI: help people use a scientific code without confusing a plain-language answer with a verified result.

In brief

  • Compute directly when the link between inputs and outputs is known. When an explicit model falls short, hybridize: keep the model and learn only what is missing. When the useful relations are not known well enough, evaluate what the data can teach, one step of complexity at a time, and only as far as it pays.
  • Simpler is often no worse, and sometimes better: on a satellite benchmark over Corsica, a learning machine that reads only its own cell forecasts better than the same model reading the whole map, with a thousand times fewer parameters, and better than the deep networks tested.
  • AI is very useful in the toolbox, not required in the solution: a language model can help prepare a calculation; the result comes from validated code.

The complexity ladder, step by step

At the center, the people who ask the question and decide. At the core, a model we understand. At the periphery, AI, one tool among others.

Pick a step. At each one, the same question: is this level enough against its reference?

1 · Compute directly

When to use it
The link between inputs and outputs is known: a physical law, a published and validated model. When no measurements exist, simulate from what is known.
Example on this site
LQL-Equiv: radiotherapy dose equivalence, computed by an explicit model (SoftwareX, 2026). Also: Tunnel Load Simulator, the energy demand of a road tunnel simulated without measurements (SoftwareX, 2026).
The reference to beat
No forecast to beat: the calculation must reproduce an independent implementation. LQL-Equiv is checked against the 2014 MATLAB version on 4,438 treatment schedules.

Is this level enough?

2 · Hybridize

When to use it
An explicit model exists but does not explain everything: keep it, and let learning estimate only what is missing.
Example on this site
Radioiodine and thyroid cancer: the compartmental model of Marie Fusella Giuntini and Dominique Barbolosi, calibrated on 50 patients (Scientific Reports, 2026), the base of a hybrid digital twin in progress.
The reference to beat
The model alone. Learning is added only where it does better than the model, on the same data.

Is this level enough?

3 · Learn frugally

When to use it
The useful relations are not known well enough, but data exist. Start with the lightest tool that does the job, such as an ELM (extreme learning machine: a small neural network whose output weights alone are fitted).
Example on this site
One ELM for the whole Corsican grid: a single model forecasts seven signals at once; one hour ahead, the normalized error (nRMSE, lower is better) is 0.179 for solar (Applied Energy, 2026).
The reference to beat
Persistence (repeat the last value), then the structured references BLEND and CLIPER and ARTU, then a statistical model. Without a clear-sky model, the ELM matches or outperforms an ARMA (autoregressive moving average) model and quantile regression (Solar Energy, 2025).

Is this level enough?

4 · AI in its place

When to use it
Deep networks, only if they beat frugal learning under the same protocol. Language models, to read a request written in plain language, never to compute.
Example on this site
WebSemantic: a language model maps the request to the inputs of a validated code, which computes every number. Rules for using AI in a lab: AI in research.
The reference to beat
The best frugal model of the previous step: on the satellite benchmark (open code), U-Net and LSTM, two deep networks, do not beat the ELM reading its own cell. For a language model, the reference is the validated code alone: the result must be the same.

Is this level enough?

5 · Demonstrate trust

When to use it
At every step, before any use: evaluate against an explicit reference, publish code, data and limits.
Example on this site
NICE: a forecast error divided by that of persistence (Sustainable Energy Technologies and Assessments, 2025); the γ-index, carried over from radiotherapy dose verification, judges a forecast with explicit tolerances (International Journal of Forecasting, 2026).
The reference to beat
Persistence. NICE = model error / persistence error: 1 means “no better than persistence”, below 1 means a gain. The RTVACOS framework covers the rest: work must be found, trusted and reused.

Numbers come from the papers cited; this page computes nothing.

One method, every field

Cyril Voyant works on the dose of a radiotherapy treatment, the response of thyroid cancer to radioactive iodine, the output of a solar plant, the electricity mix of an island, the load of a road tunnel, and requests written in plain language. Medicine, energy and language are treated the same way. The domain changes; the method does not.

1. Start with the direct approach

Read

When the link is known, compute it. Radiobiological dose equivalence follows an explicit model, checked on 4,438 treatment schedules (LQL-Equiv; SoftwareX, 2026). When no measurements exist, the electricity demand of a tunnel is simulated from what is known about it (Tunnel Load Simulator).

Before forecasting the sun, describe how variable and how forecastable a site is. Two indicators do this from the data, before any model: sCV measures the variability due to clouds, F the forecastability (sCV and F; Renewable Energy, 2026).

2. When the model falls short, hybridize

Read

This is the route Cyril Voyant prefers. When an explicit model meets the need well enough, no learning is added without reason. When it falls short, it is completed, not replaced: learning informed by physics, medicine or mathematics estimates only what the model does not capture.

  • Thyroid cancer: a compartmental model (three linked equations for the iodine, the tumor cells and a blood marker) by Marie Fusella Giuntini and Dominique Barbolosi, with Cyril Voyant and David Taieb, is the base of a hybrid digital twin now in progress (Scientific Reports, 2026).
  • Solar forecasting, 2012: hybrid forecasts combined a numerical weather model with ARMA (autoregressive moving average, a classic statistical model of time series) and neural networks (Energy, 2012).
  • Solar forecasting, 2026: cyclostationary references, which take the repeating daily cycle into account, write the symmetry of the signal into the forecast (Applied Mathematical Modelling, 2026).

3. When relations are not known well enough, learn, only as far as it pays

Read

Start with persistence, which copies the last known value. Then come structured references: CLIPER, a convex combination of persistence and the mean (positive weights adding up to 1); BLEND, a convex combination of persistence and cyclic persistence, the value one cycle earlier; ARTU, which weighs the last two values and the mean.

Next come statistical models, then frugal learning machines (ELM, extreme learning machine: a neural model whose hidden weights are drawn at random and whose output weights alone are fitted). Deep networks and foundation models, very large pre-trained models, come last. Each step is kept only if it beats the previous one under the same protocol.

  • One ELM forecasts seven signals of the Corsican grid at once, one hour ahead, with a normalized error (nRMSE, lower is better) of 0.179 for solar and 0.051 for thermal (Applied Energy, 2026).
  • Removing the clear-sky model (the irradiance expected without clouds) makes the forecasting chain simpler, and the ELM matches or outperforms the classical references (Solar Energy, 2025).

4. Artificial intelligence, in its place

Read

Machine learning, deep learning and language models are valuable and used every day. They remain tools around an understood, validated core. In WebSemantic, a language model reads the request, a validator asks whenever an input is missing, and a validated code computes every number.

Using AI in a lab also means rules: what the European AI Act asks of research, who is responsible when a model leaves the lab, and a toolbox for researchers (AI in research).

5. Trust is demonstrated

Read

Every result is judged against a reference, with metrics that match the question: NICE, which compares an error with that of persistence (Sustainable Energy Technologies and Assessments, 2025), and the γ-index, which tolerates small shifts (International Journal of Forecasting, 2026).

Each result is published with its code, data and limits, and archived with a DOI (digital object identifier, a permanent link). Research must be found, trusted and reused: this is the point of the RTVACOS framework (rigor, transparency, visibility, adoption, collaboration, outreach, societal relevance).

The same questioning applies to research itself. With J. P. A. Ioannidis (Stanford University), J. Baas and R. Boverhof (Elsevier), institutions are mapped by impact next to research-integrity proxies (Royal Society Open Science, 2026). Influence is read against an explicit 2% baseline rather than by volume (questioning how we do research).

The five principles

  1. People at the center: forecasts and calculations serve a human decision.
  2. The model at the core: understand first; compute directly; hybridize when the model falls short.
  3. Complexity only if it pays: no a priori on the method; start simple.
  4. Every tool, at the periphery: AI included, often in hybrid form.
  5. Trust is demonstrated: evaluation, open code, data and limits.
