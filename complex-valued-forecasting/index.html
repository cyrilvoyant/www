Forecasting irradiance and its uncertainty with complex numbers

A single complex autoregression, six coefficients, forecasts irradiance and its volatility at once. For a target coverage of 80%, the observed coverage stays between 79.5 and 80.7% up to six hours, and its intervals are narrower than those of quantile regression at every horizon.

The idea

Compute the clear-sky index κ = GHI/GHICS, then its volatility στ: the standard deviation of its hour-to-hour changes over the last 30 hours. Instead of forecasting one and then the other, put them in a single complex number, z(t) = κ(t) + j·στ(t), and fit an autoregression of order p to z: ẑ(t+1) = Σ ωi·z(t−i), with complex coefficients ωi. The real part predicts κ; multiplying it by GHICS recovers predicted irradiance. The imaginary part predicts the volatility of κ. The coefficients come from complex least squares, ω = (IHI + λ·1)−1IHo, with p = 6 and λ = 3.74 for Ajaccio.

Setting α

The 1 − α prediction interval is ĜHI ± μ·GHICS·σ̂. Everything lies in the multiplier μ. Under a Gaussian assumption, μ = erf−1(1 − α)/√(1 − β); but irradiance is not Gaussian, and the paper recommends calibrating μ on training data, μ = f1·ef2·α, one pair (f1, f2) per horizon. Move α: the blue curve is the calibrated μ at the chosen horizon, the dashed orange curve the Gaussian μ at 1 h.

Reading the chart: on the left, small α, almost every measurement must fall inside the interval, so μ is large and the interval wide; on the right, the reverse. The further the horizon, the higher the blue curve in general (5 and 6 h almost coincide): more σ̂ are needed for the same coverage. Below the chart, the blue band shows the resulting interval for a 500 W/m² forecast with GHICS = 800 W/m² and σ̂ = 0.10 (example values).

What to keep in mind

  • The slider evaluates μ, not a new observed coverage at each α. Table results remain those at α=0.2.
  • One site, Ajaccio, hourly daytime data; training 2008–2017, test 2018.
  • Point accuracy is of a similar order of magnitude to the other methods (nRMSE 0.196 to 0.325): the gain is in the interval.
  • On CRPS and MSIS, quantile regression remains best (MSIS 0.89 versus 0.95).
  • The appendix suggests replacing the μ(α) curve by a lookup table, an idea taken up in the probabilistic animation on the home page.
  • To compare point forecasts on public data: the forecast comparison.
