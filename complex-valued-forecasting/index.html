Forecasting irradiance and its uncertainty with complex numbers

A single complex autoregression (a forecast computed as a weighted sum of past values, with complex coefficients), six coefficients, forecasts irradiance and its volatility at once. For a target coverage of 80% (the share of measurements that should fall inside the interval), the observed coverage stays between 79.5 and 80.7% up to six hours, and its intervals are narrower than those of quantile regression (the reference method that estimates an interval directly) at every horizon. You will see here how a prediction interval is set, and why it opens when the sky becomes unstable.

The idea: one number for two quantities

Start from κ = GHI/GHICS, the ratio to the clear-sky trend, or clear-sky index: measured irradiance GHI divided by that of a cloudless sky GHICS. Its volatility στ is the standard deviation of its hour-to-hour changes over the last τ = 30 hours. Instead of forecasting one and then the other, put them together: z = κ + j·στ, where j is the imaginary unit (j² = −1).

The forecast is an autoregression: a sum of the latest values of z, each multiplied by a complex coefficient. The complex product does the work: it mixes real and imaginary parts, so κ and its volatility inform each other with very few parameters. The real part of the forecast, multiplied by GHICS, gives the forecast irradiance ĜHI; the imaginary part gives the forecast volatility σ̂. The coefficients are computed by complex least squares, with a regularization that limits their size (λ = 3.74, the value giving the lowest error at Ajaccio).

Setting the interval

Explore interval coverage and width. Probabilistic solar forecasting.

The prediction interval is ĜHI ± μ·GHICS·σ̂. It aims to contain a share 1 − α of the measurements. Everything lies in the multiplier μ. If errors are assumed Gaussian, μ can be calculated; but this assumption does not hold for irradiance, and the paper advises calibrating μ on training data. Choose a question, or move the sliders.

On the left, small α: almost every measurement must fall inside the interval, so μ is large. The further the horizon, the higher the blue curve in general (5 and 6 h almost coincide): more σ̂ are needed for the same coverage.

What each quantity means

α
the share of measurements allowed outside the interval. α = 0.2: 80% of measurements are targeted inside.
μ
the number of σ̂ on each side of the forecast. It does not depend on σ̂: the σ̂ slider only moves the band.
Calibrated μ
μ = f1·ef2·α, with two constants fitted on 2008–2017 for each horizon.
Gaussian μ
μ = erf−1(1 − α)/√(1 − β), where erf−1 comes from the normal law and β = 0.38 measures how much the fast fluctuations of κ resemble each other from one hour to the next.
σ̂
the forecast volatility, without unit. The slider gives example values; in the method, it comes out of the autoregression at each time.
Band
an example with ĜHI = 500 W/m² and GHICS = 800 W/m², the vertical mark on the right. The blue band follows the calibrated μ, the orange frame the Gaussian μ at 1 h.

The paper's results: at 80% target, the right coverage with the shortest intervals

The complex method and quantile regression hold the 80% target. The Gaussian model exceeds it by paying with wider intervals. Bootstrap sometimes gives shorter intervals, but falls as low as 73% coverage: a short interval that misses its target is worthless.

What to keep in mind

  • One site, Ajaccio, hourly daytime data; training on 2008–2017, test on 2018. Measured results are for α = 0.2; for other α, the slider computes μ, not an observed coverage.
  • The accuracy of the forecast itself is of a similar order to the other methods (nRMSE 0.196 to 0.325): the gain is in the interval.
  • On the overall scores of the distribution and the interval, CRPS and MSIS, where lower is better, quantile regression stays ahead (MSIS 0.89 versus 0.95).
